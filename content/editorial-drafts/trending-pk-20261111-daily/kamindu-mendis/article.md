Kamindu is a Sri Lankan left-handed batter whose ability to bowl spin with either arm drew attention during his youth career. An ICC feature published in January 2018 documented that skill while he was involved in under-19 cricket. His later recognition at Test level came through sustained batting performances, not simply the novelty of switching bowling arms.

That distinction matters when judging an all-rounder. A striking secondary skill can make a short video popular, but selection and match contribution depend on what a player can deliver repeatedly. For a beginner, an all-rounder is a player who offers meaningful batting and bowling options. The label alone does not tell you the balance between those jobs. Look at actual use in the match: where the player bats, whether he receives a bowling spell and what responsibilities the team gives him.

## Why a middle-order fifty can matter

The middle order often meets a match already shaped by other players. Sometimes the openers have made a strong start. Sometimes several wickets have fallen and the fielding side is attacking. An identical individual score can serve different purposes in those two situations.

Imagine two hypothetical innings of fifty. One comes after an opening partnership of 200; the other begins with three early wickets down. Neither should automatically be dismissed as easy or difficult, because conditions and bowling quality also matter. But the second batter may have to accept a period of low scoring to keep the innings intact.

Partnership building is therefore more informative than counting boundaries alone. A batter can help by leaving deliveries outside the scoring area, defending well, taking a safe single or allowing a settled partner to retain strike. These actions do not all produce dramatic highlights, yet they can change the contest's rhythm.

This is analysis of the role, not a claim about the intention behind every delivery in Kamindu's innings. A scorecard records outcomes. Understanding decision-making in detail would require ball-by-ball evidence and footage, rather than assumptions based only on the final total.

## How to read runs, balls and boundaries

A batting row usually separates runs scored from deliveries faced. Balls faced help describe the innings' length, while boundary counts show one way the runs were accumulated. None of those figures, by itself, establishes whether an innings was appropriate for the match situation.

For a simple invented example, suppose a batter makes 60 from 120 balls. The strike rate is 60 divided by 120, multiplied by 100: 50 runs per hundred balls. This does not mean he scored exactly fifty runs in every block of a hundred deliveries. It is a rate calculated across the whole innings.

Now suppose that innings includes six fours. Those boundaries contribute 24 runs; the remaining 36 must come through other scoring shots, assuming no other boundaries. The calculation helps a learner distinguish boundary scoring from running between the wickets. It is an illustration, not an alternative account of the Rawalpindi innings.

Do not apply a T20 expectation automatically to a Test. A Test batter may need to negotiate a difficult spell before increasing his scoring rate. Equally, occupying the crease is not the only objective. The balance depends on wickets available, time, conditions and the team's position.

## What “declared”, “not out” and “trail” mean

“Declared” means the batting captain has voluntarily closed the innings. It is not the same as the side losing all ten wickets. The decision can create more time to bowl at the opposition, although the score alone cannot prove the captain's full tactical reasoning.

“Not out” identifies a batter whose innings has not ended through dismissal. It is particularly important when comparing batting averages. The usual average divides total runs by dismissals, not by matches played and not simply by the number of times the player came to the crease.

Consider a hypothetical player with scores of 40 not out, 20 and 60. He has made 120 runs and been dismissed twice, producing an average of 60. Dividing by three would calculate runs per innings instead. Both calculations use the same scores but answer different questions.

“Trail” compares the two sides' totals at that stage. Being behind after the opposition's first innings does not mean the match has been lost. Conversely, passing that total does not automatically mean victory. Test cricket allows more than one innings, and the match still has to reach a result within the available play.

## How does bowling with both arms work?

Ambidextrous bowling means using either arm to deliver the ball. Kamindu's youth profile describes both right-arm and left-arm spin. It does not mean that he bats with both hands interchangeably, and it does not mean the two deliveries have identical angles or movement.

For a learner, place the bowler and batter on a simple sketch of the pitch. Changing the delivery arm can change the line from which the ball approaches. Changing the bowler's side of the wicket introduces another variable. Neither change guarantees a wicket; control of length and the batter's response still matter.

There is an important difference between having an option and using it effectively. A bowler needs repeatable accuracy with each method. A captain must also decide whether that option fits the conditions, the batter and the match plan. The unusual skill is an extra tactical possibility, rather than a shortcut around those requirements.

## Watch a partnership rather than only a personal total

An individual score tells you what one batter contributed. A partnership tells you what happened while two batters shared the crease. It can include extras as well as their own runs, which is why adding their individual scores does not always reproduce the partnership figure.

Try this invented exercise: a new pair starts at 100 for three and the next wicket falls at 175. The partnership is 75. If one batter scores 40 during that period and the other scores 30, five runs must be accounted for elsewhere in the partnership, such as extras. The exercise demonstrates the bookkeeping; it is not a substitute scorecard for this Test.

The same approach helps explain why an early contribution and a later large stand are connected without being the same partnership. When a wicket falls, a new partnership begins. Giving every run in a recovery to the batter with the largest score oversimplifies the sequence.
