## Why seven wickets is the correct margin

## Converting 7.4 overs into legal balls

For ordinary cricket over notation, the digit after the dot counts legal balls within the incomplete over. Beardman's 7.4 therefore corresponds to seven completed overs plus four legal balls. The calculation is seven multiplied by six, then four added: 46 legal balls.

Treating 7.4 as a decimal number of overs would produce a different and incorrect denominator for this calculation. In cricket notation, the incomplete-over digit is not a tenth of an over. That is why the notation needs conversion before it is used in a calculator or spreadsheet formula.

For another example, 10.2 overs represents 62 legal balls, not 61.2. Once the notation is converted correctly, comparisons such as runs per over and balls per wicket become easier to calculate consistently. The important first step is understanding what the symbol means before applying arithmetic.

## What Beardman's economy calculation shows

Using the recorded 24 runs and 46 legal balls, the economy calculation is 24 multiplied by six, divided by 46. That is approximately 3.13 runs per over. The unit matters: it is an innings rate, not the number of runs conceded in every individual over.

The bowling average for this performance is 24 divided by six, which equals four runs per wicket. The bowling strike rate is 46 divided by six, or approximately 7.67 legal balls per wicket. These are three different measurements produced from the same return, and none should be substituted for another.

A low economy figure describes run control through that innings calculation. A low bowling strike-rate figure describes how frequently wickets arrived on average.
