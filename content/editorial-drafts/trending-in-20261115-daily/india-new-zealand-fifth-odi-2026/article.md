## India beat New Zealand by 22 runs in the fifth ODI

India defended 209 against New Zealand at Bay Oval, Mount Maunganui, on 15 November 2026, winning the fifth and final one-day international by 22 runs. India were dismissed in 42.1 overs; New Zealand were all out for 187 in 48.1 overs. The result gave India the five-match ODI series 3-1 because the third game had no result. The [BCCI match listing](https://www.bcci.tv/matches) confirms the final totals and margin. The series score matters as much as the day’s score: New Zealand had won the fourth ODI by 244 runs at the same ground, so the finale tested whether India could respond with the ball after another difficult batting innings.

This guide brings the result, key individual contributions and the series sequence together. A short score headline can obscure how close a chase of 210 became when the lower order survived deep into the innings. Equally, a 22-run margin should not be described as an easy defence merely because the target looked modest. India needed ten wickets; New Zealand still had deliveries available when the last batter fell. The precise facts below are separated from interpretation so that readers can check the scorecard without treating tactical inferences as recorded events.

### Why this was more than another low total

India’s 209 was not a commanding first-innings score in a 50-over game. They had nearly eight overs left unused when the last wicket fell. That fact matters because a side that uses all 50 overs can sometimes turn a fragile position into an additional 20 or 30 runs through singles and lower-order partnerships. Here, India had to protect what they had. New Zealand needed 210 to win, not 209, and they could in principle have taken the chase into the final over. India’s bowlers therefore had to create dismissals rather than merely wait for time to run out.

The fourth ODI made the situation sharper. Two days earlier, New Zealand had posted 333 and dismissed India for 89 at Bay Oval. The [ICC account of that match](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/young-henry-star-as-new-zealand-keep-india-odi-series-alive) confirms the 244-run defeat, Will Young’s 125 and Matt Henry’s six wickets. A five-match series that had seemed within India’s control was suddenly alive for the hosts. The fifth match was not literally a winner-takes-all final: India came in ahead because they had won the first two games and the third was washed out. But if New Zealand won the fifth, the series would be level at 2-2. India’s victory instead turned the unfinished series question into a 3-1 result.

The venue was the same, but the score pattern changed completely. It would be simplistic to say Bay Oval is automatically a high-scoring or low-scoring ground based on two matches. Different players, bowling spells, starts and match conditions produce different totals. The useful comparison is narrower: New Zealand had dominated the fourth ODI with both bat and ball, whereas India successfully defended a much smaller total in the fifth. That reversal is why the finale deserves its own result page rather than being folded into the earlier fourth-ODI report.

### How India reached 209

New Zealand captain Mitchell Santner chose to bowl after winning the toss, according to the match account. India’s opening phase did not provide a platform. Rohit Sharma made seven, Virat Kohli five and Ruturaj Gaikwad 18. By the time Gaikwad was dismissed, India were 34/3 in the ninth over. The third early wicket changed the shape of the innings: the middle order had to recover before it could think about setting an ambitious target. A score of 34/3 does not by itself prove that every batter made an avoidable error; it records the position India had to repair.

Axar Patel and captain Shreyas Iyer rebuilt from that position. Iyer’s 22 was a supporting contribution rather than a large score, but it helped take the innings away from an immediate collapse. Axar’s 46 from 55 balls supplied one of India’s two joint-highest individual scores. He was out when the total reached 121/5 in the 25th over. Reaching 121 by halfway left a plausible route to a higher target, yet it required the remaining batters to stay in. New Zealand’s bowlers prevented that route from becoming a sustained acceleration.

Ishan Kishan also made 46, from 58 balls, while Naman Dhir supplied a quicker 31 from 22 deliveries. Their contributions pushed India beyond 150, but New Zealand continued to strike. India were 166/8 after 36 overs and 183/9 in the 41st. Those positions show why the final 209 was valuable even though it was below what teams often seek in an ODI. Arshdeep Singh struck 14 from four balls, including two sixes, while Mohammed Siraj finished 14 not out. The last partnership’s runs were part of the target India later defended. They did not make the innings strong, but they raised the chase from a potentially easier number into the 210-run target New Zealand faced.

Santner’s 3/47 and Duffy’s 3/60 were the major wicket returns for New Zealand, with Matthew Fisher taking two wickets for 28 runs. The figures should be read in context. Duffy’s three wickets were crucial, but his 60 runs conceded were not the same economy as Santner’s ten-over spell. A bowler’s wickets and runs are separate measurements; both help explain the innings. The record also shows that India’s main batting contributions were spread across several players rather than anchored by a century or even a half-century.

### The chase: early restraint, then wickets

New Zealand began chasing 210 with enough overs to score at just over four runs per over. That arithmetic can make a pursuit appear comfortable, but the required run rate was never the only question. India needed wickets, and the home side could not afford to trade too many of them for a slow start. Siraj removed Tim Seifert for 14, leaving New Zealand 26/1 in the sixth over. Will Young, whose century had driven the previous match, made 22 in this one. Arshdeep dismissed him, and the home side were 64/2 in the 17th over.

At 80/2 after 20 overs, New Zealand still had time. The decisive pressure came when Arshdeep took Nick Kelly and Finn Allen in a short span. From 80/2, the chase became 83/4. A reader looking only at the run rate might miss that shift. Four wickets down changes which batters can take risks and how much the lower order may have to do. It also creates space for the fielding side to attack rather than defend a boundary every ball. This is an interpretation of the score position, not a claim that India used one specific field setting throughout.

Dean Foxcroft was New Zealand’s highest scorer with 31. Michael Bracewell made 19. The pair kept the home side in the game through the middle overs; New Zealand were 116/4 after 30. But Auqib Nabi and Yash Thakur removed Bracewell and Foxcroft, taking the score to 119/6. The scoring rate remained manageable in theory, while the loss of wickets narrowed the margin for error. For a chase of 210, that was a critical distinction. The target was not racing away; the available partnerships were.

Santner made 24 and Kristian Clarke 16. Their stand took New Zealand to 155/6 after 40 overs, leaving 55 from the last ten. That was still mathematically achievable. Naman Dhir removed Santner, and Arshdeep dismissed Clarke. At 161/8, New Zealand needed late runs from its tail. The match went into the 49th over because Blair Tickner and the remaining batters kept the innings alive. Axar eventually took the final wicket at 187 in 48.1 overs, closing out the 22-run win. The late survival explains why the defence was a contest rather than a foregone conclusion after the middle-overs wickets.

Arshdeep finished with 3/30, Axar 2/28 and Siraj 2/50. Auqib Nabi, Yash Thakur and Naman Dhir each contributed a wicket in the detailed account. Six wicket takers in a single defence illustrate a collective effort; the figures do not require an invented story about one bowler carrying every phase. Arshdeep’s three wickets stand out because they included the important middle-over strikes as well as Clarke’s late dismissal. Axar’s two wickets mattered at the end, while his earlier 46 made him a contributor with both bat and ball.

### How to read the 3-1 series result

The five scheduled ODI dates did not produce five wins and losses. India won the first ODI, won the second under the DLS method, the third produced no result, New Zealand won the fourth, and India won the fifth. That adds up to India 3, New Zealand 1, one no result. The series score is therefore **3-1**, not 3-2 and not 2-2. A “no result” match counts as a scheduled fixture but does not give either team a win. This is an important point for readers comparing a five-match programme with a four-decision series line.

The second ODI’s DLS finish also explains why some score summaries look unusual if compared without context. DLS is used to set or revise a target when rain interrupts a limited-overs match. It is not an extra match or a bonus point. India’s first two victories were enough to ensure that New Zealand could at best draw the series after the third washout. The fourth ODI gave the hosts that chance, and the fifth removed it. The result at Bay Oval therefore settled the series, rather than simply adding one more match to an already fixed margin.

PTI reported that the victory ended New Zealand’s 11-series unbeaten run at home in ODIs. That is a reported historical claim, not something the fifth-ODI scorecard alone can establish. The scorecard proves this match and, alongside the preceding fixtures, the 3-1 series outcome. A broader streak requires a separate historical series record. We mention the reported streak as context and keep it distinct from the independently checkable totals. The same care is useful when claims about “biggest” or “first in years” appear immediately after a high-profile result.

### What the figures do and do not show

The final score is straightforward: 209 minus 187 equals a 22-run margin. The meaning of the bowling figures needs a little care. Arshdeep’s 3/30 means three wickets for 30 runs conceded; it is not a batting score. Santner’s 3/47 refers to New Zealand’s bowling against India, while his 24 is a batting contribution in the chase. Axar’s 46 and 2/28 similarly come from two different innings. Reading each statistic in its role prevents a common mistake when compact scorecards place names and numbers close together.

Overs are written in cricket notation rather than decimal notation. India’s 42.1 overs means 42 complete overs and one ball of the next, not 42.1 decimal overs. New Zealand’s 48.1 means 48 overs plus one ball. This matters when someone tries to calculate run rates or remaining balls. A 50-over innings has 300 legal balls available; India used 253 and New Zealand used 289. The chase ended when New Zealand lost their tenth wicket, with eleven legal deliveries left in the scheduled innings. Those numbers follow directly from the overs notation and help explain why India had to bowl New Zealand out rather than merely defend the target until the overs expired.

An all-out total is the team’s finished innings. India’s 209/10 and New Zealand’s 187/10 are sometimes shortened to 209 and 187 in score listings. They mean the same final totals here. The winner is determined by the team runs in this completed match, not by comparing only the highest individual batter, number of sixes or one bowler’s wickets. Ishan and Axar sharing India’s top score of 46 did not make the innings a tie. Foxcroft’s 31 was the highest New Zealand individual score, yet the team total and wickets decided the match.

#### Why did New Zealand lose despite needing only 210?

The target was achievable in terms of runs and balls, but New Zealand lost all ten wickets for 187. India’s wickets in the middle overs and again late in the chase prevented a winning partnership. A low required run rate cannot compensate for losing the final wicket.

### The final reading

India did not need a large batting total to win the series finale. Axar and Ishan’s 46s helped create a defendable 209, and a group of bowlers then reduced New Zealand to 187. Arshdeep’s 3/30 was the standout wicket return, while Axar added two wickets to his batting contribution. The final margin was 22 runs and the series ended 3-1. The fourth ODI had shown how badly India could be beaten at Bay Oval; the fifth showed the opposite outcome on the scoreboard without erasing that earlier loss.
