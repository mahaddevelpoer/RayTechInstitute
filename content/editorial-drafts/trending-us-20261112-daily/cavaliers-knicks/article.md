## Why more made shots did not mean more points

The reviewed team totals show Cleveland with 40 field goals, including 15 threes, plus 20 made free throws. New York had 41 field goals, including 14 threes, plus 11 made free throws. Those categories account for the reported final totals.

For Cleveland, subtract the 15 threes from 40 field goals to obtain 25 two-point baskets. That gives 50 points from twos, 45 from threes and 20 from free throws: 115. For New York, 27 twos produce 54, 14 threes produce 42 and the free throws add 11: 107.

The calculation shows the scoring composition. It does not, on its own, explain why each opportunity occurred or whether every decision was optimal. A numerical reconciliation is a useful first step, not a complete tactical verdict.

Keeping categories separate also prevents a common counting mistake: three-pointers are included within total field goals. Adding all field goals as twos and then adding the full three-point total again would double-count part of the scoring.

## Harden's points can be reconciled in the same way

The reviewed line gives Harden nine made field goals, five of them threes, and nine made free throws. Four two-point baskets contribute eight points; five threes contribute 15; the free throws contribute nine. The sum is 32.

That is a clearer explanation than assuming nine field goals must mean 18 points. Basketball scoring uses different values, so the total depends on the composition of the makes. The same method can check any player's points when the necessary categories are available.

Do not treat the reconciliation as proof that every part of a performance was equally strong. Scoring is one contribution. Playmaking, possessions used and defensive work raise different questions that require their own evidence.

A student can reproduce the calculation in a spreadsheet with columns for twos, threes and free throws. Keep the inputs visible rather than entering only a final total. Another reader can then identify whether an error came from the source number or the formula.

## Percentage and value answer different questions

A shooting percentage describes makes relative to attempts in the specified category. It does not automatically account for the different value of the shots. A higher field-goal percentage can coexist with fewer total points when the scoring mix and other opportunities differ.

Consider an original example unrelated to this game. Team A makes five two-point shots from ten attempts, scoring ten points. Team B makes four three-point shots from ten attempts, scoring 12. The second team has the lower ordinary field-goal percentage in this small example but the higher scoring return.

The example does not say that every team should take only threes. Shot difficulty, player skill, available space and the consequences of misses matter. It demonstrates a narrower point: a percentage needs to be interpreted alongside what is being counted.

When comparing statistics, write the numerator and denominator in words. Are you comparing made shots with attempts, points with possessions or one player's share of team activity? Clear units prevent a visually impressive number from being used to answer the wrong question.

## Free throws belong in the explanation without becoming the entire explanation

Free throws add points outside the field-goal total. An account that compares only baskets can therefore miss a substantial part of the score. Include made free throws when reconciling points, while keeping attempts and percentage as separate fields.

More free throws can arise within different game situations. A short box-score summary alone does not establish every reason for the difference. Avoid turning the numerical gap into an accusation about officiating or a complete description of attacking style without the relevant evidence.

An original arithmetic task can make the distinction clear. Two imaginary teams each score 80 points from field goals. If one adds 15 free throws and the other adds nine, their totals become 95 and 89. The example isolates the accounting effect; it does not reconstruct the Cavaliers–Knicks game.

For a full match review, the next step would be to examine the timing and circumstances of actual attempts. That is a different task from explaining why the final totals add up. Both can be useful when their scope is stated honestly.

## Read minutes before comparing raw totals

A player's opportunity to accumulate points or rebounds depends partly on time on court. That does not make a total unimportant, but it gives the comparison context. A short appearance and a long appearance should not be treated as identical samples.

Minutes themselves need careful notation. A line such as 33:26 means 33 minutes and 26 seconds, not an ordinary decimal number of minutes. If calculating a rate, convert the seconds consistently before dividing.

For an illustrative example, 12 points in 20 minutes equals 0.6 points per minute. Multiplying that rate by a longer period is a mathematical projection, not a prediction that the player would necessarily sustain it with a different workload or opponent.

Label such exercises as derived measures. Do not advertise a projection as an official achievement or use it to erase the actual conditions in which the recorded performance occurred. A calculation can clarify a sample without replacing it.

## A team result cannot be assigned to one column

Points determine the winner, but a team's opportunities are shaped through many actions. A rebound can end or extend a sequence. A turnover can remove a chance to score. An assist describes a credited contribution to a made basket, while other useful decisions may not receive an assist.

These relationships are reasons to look beyond a single headline statistic, not permission to invent a causal account of this game. If a report does not establish how a particular run occurred, leave that sequence unresolved rather than supplying a familiar basketball explanation.

An effective notebook separates recorded facts from questions. Put the final score and the verified player line in the first column. In the second, write what you would need to inspect next, such as a scoring stretch or lineup interval. That keeps curiosity from becoming an unsupported conclusion.

The same discipline improves comparisons between players. One may have a larger scoring role while another contributes through a different assignment. Identify the role actually performed before reducing the discussion to a ranking based on one number.

## Understand the limits of a single-game plus-minus

A plus-minus figure connects a player's time on court with the team's scoring difference during those minutes. It does not isolate the player from teammates, opponents or the timing of substitutions. Reading it as a complete individual rating would give it more meaning than it can support alone.

Imagine two players sharing almost all their minutes. Their scoring environment will overlap substantially. A positive team stretch can appear in both lines without establishing that one person independently caused every basket or stop.

For a more complete assessment, a reviewer would need additional context and a suitable sample. This guide does not claim to have performed that analysis. It explains why a number should remain attached to the situation it measures.

That restraint is compatible with recognising a strong scoring performance. Appreciating Harden's recorded 32 points does not require pretending that every other statistic is a final verdict on his entire contribution or on every other player.

## A practical box-score worksheet

Create columns for date, teams, final status and source. Then add the shooting categories needed to reconcile points. Calculate two-point makes from total field goals minus three-point makes, and retain free throws as a separate contribution.

Use a second section for your own calculations. Give each formula a descriptive label and show the inputs. If another reader obtains a different result, the visible structure makes it easier to find the difference without arguing about an unexplained final number.

Leave a final section for interpretation. Write a sentence about what the calculation establishes, followed by a sentence about what it does not establish. For this game, the scoring composition explains how the team totals reconcile; it does not identify the cause of every shot opportunity.

This is a useful numeracy exercise precisely because the answer can be checked. The goal is not to make the statistics sound more sophisticated than they are, but to keep the arithmetic, units and evidence understandable.

## Do not average percentages without their attempt counts

An original arithmetic example shows the problem. One imaginary player makes one shot from two attempts, while another makes nine from ten. Their individual percentages are 50 and 90. Taking the ordinary average gives 70, but their combined shooting is ten makes from 12 attempts, approximately 83.3 percent.

The difference comes from the unequal number of opportunities. Each attempt contributes to the combined total; the two individual percentages do not represent equally sized samples. This is a general numeracy lesson, not an additional statistic from the Cavaliers–Knicks fixture.

When calculating a team percentage from player lines, add the makes and add the attempts before dividing. Keep the category consistent: do not combine one player's three-point attempts with another player's total field-goal attempts and label the result a single shooting percentage.

The same caution applies when comparing games. A strong percentage from a very small sample should not be treated as equally informative as a much larger sample without explanation. The number can be accurate while the comparison remains poorly framed. Retaining the denominator is a simple way to make the reasoning visible.
