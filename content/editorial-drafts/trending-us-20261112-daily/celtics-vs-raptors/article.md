## Start by reconciling the 24 points

Field goals include both two-point and three-point makes. If a player makes six field goals and two of those are threes, the remaining four are twos. That distinction is the first step toward reconstructing the scoring total; counting all six as two-pointers and forgetting the additional value of the threes would be incomplete.

Four two-point baskets contribute eight points. Two three-point baskets contribute six. Ten made free throws contribute ten more. Add eight, six and ten, and the result is 24. The calculation agrees with the completed scoring line, which is a useful check that the categories have been interpreted correctly.

The same calculation can be written in another form: two times all made field goals, plus the made threes, plus made free throws. That gives twelve plus two plus ten. The extra two accounts for the additional point on each three. Both methods work because they describe the same scoring structure.

## Why 6-for-21 is not the whole offensive account

Six divided by 21 is approximately 28.6 percent. That describes the proportion of field-goal attempts made. It does not include free throws, and it does not give a three-point make more weight than a two-point make. The statistic answers a specific question rather than summarising every form of scoring contribution.

Tatum's ten made free throws supplied ten of his 24 points in this completed line. Ten divided by 24 is approximately 41.7 percent. That is the share of his points coming from the foul line, not his free-throw percentage and not his share of Boston's total points. Three different denominators would produce three different measures.

This is the central lesson of the game-stat exercise: a percentage needs a sentence explaining what is being divided by what. A low field-goal percentage and a meaningful points total can coexist because points arrive through more than one category. That observation does not make missed shots irrelevant; it keeps the description complete.

## Separate two-point and three-point accuracy

The completed line includes two made threes from 11 attempts. Subtract those attempts from 21 total field-goal attempts, and ten attempts remain inside the two-point category. Subtract the two made threes from six total makes, and four made twos remain. The two-point percentage is therefore four divided by ten, or 40 percent.

The three-point percentage is two divided by 11, approximately 18.2 percent. The combined field-goal percentage is not the simple average of 40 and 18.2 because the attempt counts differ. It is the total six makes divided by the total 21 attempts. A weighted relationship, rather than an unweighted average, connects the categories.

For a classroom check, multiply each category's exact rate by its number of attempts to recover the number of makes. Use the original fractions rather than rounded display percentages. Rounding is useful for presentation but can introduce small discrepancies when a displayed percentage is treated as an exact input.

## A simple weighted-scoring measure

One common way to recognise the extra value of threes is effective field-goal percentage. For this educational calculation, add half the made threes to made field goals, then divide by field-goal attempts. The extra half reflects that a three is worth one and a half times a two.

Using the completed line, six plus half of two gives seven. Seven divided by 21 is one third, or approximately 33.3 percent. This calculation weights field-goal scoring differently from ordinary field-goal percentage. It still does not include free throws, so it should not be described as a complete measure of every offensive contribution.

The purpose is not to select the one number that makes a performance look best or worst. Each measure has a definition. A useful explanation states that definition, calculates it consistently and describes its limits. A table containing several well-labelled measures is more informative than an unexplained ranking based on one of them.

## Read the foul-line percentage separately

It is different from the earlier 41.7 percent figure describing the share of Tatum's points supplied by free throws. A five-point win describes the difference at the final buzzer. A 22-point deficit describes an earlier game state in the reported account. Neither number cancels the other. Together they tell a more specific story: the winning team had to recover from a substantially worse position before establishing its eventual advantage.

That arithmetic describes the change in relative score, not a claim that the team scored twelve unanswered points. The opponent could also have scored during the recovery.

Apply the same distinction whenever a headline uses the word “comeback.” Ask which checkpoint defines the starting margin and whether the writer is describing a run or an overall change. ## Rebounds and assists are not extra points

The completed Tatum line includes seven rebounds and eight assists. Those categories describe contributions beyond his own scoring, but they should not be added to 24 to create an invented points total. Different statistical units can be discussed together without being combined into a meaningless sum.

An assist is connected to a teammate's made basket under the scoring convention. It is not automatically worth a fixed number of points in an individual's line. A rebound records recovery of the ball after a missed attempt, with further distinctions depending on the category. Understanding the action is more useful than treating every box-score number as interchangeable.
