IndiGo share-price searches refer to InterGlobe Aviation Limited, the airline company, not the price of an individual flight ticket or another business using the Indigo name. Its official investor-relations page is the starting point for financial and operational disclosures. A current stock quote needs an exchange timestamp; this guide does not invent a live rate or a price target.

Airlines are easier to understand when capacity, passengers, revenue and cost are kept separate. A fuller aircraft does not automatically mean a more profitable flight, and a larger schedule does not by itself establish stronger cash generation. This article explains those relationships using clearly labelled examples rather than unverified current operating figures.

Identify InterGlobe Aviation Limited before recording a share price. A passenger-facing brand, a listed company and an unrelated international company can share similar words in search results. Their accounts and market quotes are not interchangeable merely because the page title contains Indigo.

## Seats and passengers are different counts

A scheduled aircraft can offer seats without every seat being occupied. Capacity describes the available opportunity, while traffic describes use under the relevant measure. The distinction is fundamental to airline analysis and should remain visible in any comparison.

An invented example has 100 available seats and 80 passengers on one simple flight. The occupancy calculation for that flight is 80 percent under those assumptions. It does not tell us the fare paid, the journey distance or the profit. Those require additional information.

Do not treat a photograph of a busy terminal as a company-wide traffic statistic. A crowded gate can be real while representing one moment and one location. Official operating disclosures are the appropriate source for defined traffic measures over a period.

## What are available seat kilometres?

Available seat kilometres combine the number of available seats with distance. In a simplified example, 100 seats flown over 1,000 kilometres produce 100,000 available seat kilometres. This is a capacity measure, not a passenger count or a revenue amount.

Distance matters because two schedules can offer the same number of seats while covering different lengths. A short flight and a long flight are not identical capacity contributions under this measure. Counting departures alone therefore cannot reproduce the available-seat-kilometre total.

The example is not IndiGo's capacity data. It provides a way to read a disclosed metric correctly. Use the company's actual definition and reporting period for real values, and avoid combining a monthly traffic figure with a quarterly capacity figure as though they cover the same interval.

## Revenue passenger kilometres and load factor

Revenue passenger kilometres measure paying passenger traffic with distance under the relevant reporting definition. Comparing that traffic with available seat kilometres produces a distance-weighted load-factor measure. It is not simply an unweighted average of every flight's seat occupancy.

For a fictional example, 80 paying passengers travelling 1,000 kilometres produce 80,000 passenger kilometres. Against the 100,000 available seat kilometres above, the ratio is 80 percent. The calculation makes the units and denominator explicit rather than treating load factor as a free-standing score.

Now imagine a network with flights of different sizes and lengths. A simple average of two percentages may not match the properly weighted network figure. The underlying capacity and traffic totals matter. This is the same statistical lesson encountered when combining exam results from classes with different numbers of students.

## A high load factor does not establish profit

Occupancy tells a reader how capacity was used under the measure. It does not disclose the fares, ancillary income or costs associated with that activity. A full flight can have different economics from another full flight, and a less-full flight can still require a broader network context.

In an invented model, one flight carries 100 passengers paying one unit each, while another carries 80 paying two units each. Their simplified ticket revenues are 100 and 160. The first is fuller under the assumed equal seat capacity, but the second earns more ticket revenue in the example.

These are not actual fares or a recommendation for pricing. They demonstrate why an article should not convert occupancy directly into a profit claim. A useful airline analysis reads traffic, revenue and cost together rather than selecting one visually impressive metric.

## Yield and revenue per capacity unit

Airline reporting can use measures relating passenger revenue to traffic and revenue to available capacity. Their numerators and denominators differ. Read the company's labels and definitions before comparing a yield measure with revenue per available seat kilometre or another unit-revenue figure.

The practical question is what each ratio is intended to explain. A traffic-based ratio asks about revenue relative to the traffic carried. A capacity-based ratio asks about revenue relative to the capacity offered. They can change differently when occupancy changes, even without an arithmetic error.

For students, create a fictional table with capacity, paying traffic and revenue. Calculate two separately named ratios and then change the traffic while holding capacity constant. The exercise shows why a single unlabeled unit-revenue number can hide important information about the business.

## Cost per unit of capacity needs its scope

A cost-per-available-seat-kilometre measure relates a defined cost total to capacity. Some presentations distinguish fuel or other components, so the label and notes matter. A cost figure excluding one component should not be compared with a total-cost figure as though they were identical.

In a fictional model, 50 cost units spread over 100 capacity units produce 0.5 per unit. If capacity increases to 125 while the same simplified cost remains, the ratio becomes 0.4. The example shows a denominator effect; it does not establish the company's actual costs or future efficiency.

Real airline costs do not all remain fixed when a schedule changes. The mix of fixed and variable elements matters. A comparison should use actual disclosures and clearly stated assumptions instead of extrapolating a simple classroom model into a guaranteed margin improvement.

## Fuel prices are relevant, but not a complete earnings forecast

Fuel can be an important airline cost, but a public oil benchmark is not automatically the airline's actual fuel expense per period. Contracting, timing, currency and the specific product can affect the relationship. The financial statements provide the recognised cost, while a market quotation supplies a different reference.

Do not multiply a guessed consumption amount by today's benchmark and present the result as the company's reported bill. The missing inputs are part of the problem, not an invitation to fill the gaps with convenient assumptions. A transparent hypothetical exercise must remain labelled as such.

A careful discussion can explain why fuel headlines attract attention while leaving the exact company effect to the relevant disclosures. That is more informative than asserting a fixed profit change for every movement in a reference price without a documented model.

## Currency, leases and cash timing

Airlines can have financial items exposed to currencies and payment schedules different from their reporting presentation. Accounting expenses, cash payments and translation effects may not occur in the same way or period. Use the actual notes rather than assume every reported change is an immediate operating cash movement.

For an original fictional illustration, a payment denominated in 100 foreign units converts into different reporting-currency amounts at two hypothetical rates. The foreign amount is unchanged while the converted amount differs. This explains a mechanism without claiming a particular exposure or hedge for IndiGo.

The same discipline applies to lease-related figures. A cash payment and an accounting expense should not be treated as synonyms without reading their definitions. This guide does not reconstruct the company's contracts; it identifies why the relevant statement notes belong in the analysis.

## Aircraft availability and schedule capacity

A fleet count does not automatically equal aircraft available for every scheduled flight. Maintenance, operational arrangements and other disclosed factors can affect availability. A headline about fleet size should therefore be read separately from the period's actual operated capacity.

Do not invent a current grounding count or technical cause from an old article. A historical maintenance issue can remain online after its status changes. If availability is central to a current earnings discussion, use the company's dated disclosure and preserve the reference period.

For a learning exercise, model a fictional fleet with total aircraft, available aircraft and scheduled utilisation as separate inputs. Changing one does not necessarily change all the others by the same percentage. The exercise explains the categories without providing operational instructions or claims about the current fleet.

## Route mix and seasonality affect comparisons

A network can contain routes with different distances, demand patterns and pricing conditions. A change in mix can affect average metrics even when individual routes behave differently. A quarter-to-quarter comparison should therefore be read with the reporting period and the company's disclosed context.

Seasonal demand is another reason to distinguish year-on-year and sequential comparisons. They answer different questions. Neither is automatically the better measure for every purpose, and a writer should not switch between them without explanation to make a trend appear stronger.

Use matching periods and definitions in a worksheet. If a measure changes scope, note it before drawing a continuous line. A polished chart cannot repair incompatible inputs, and a percentage without its base period can be less useful than a clearly labelled raw figure.

## Revenue, profit and cash are separate outcomes

Higher traffic can coexist with different revenue or cost outcomes depending on the actual circumstances. Revenue growth does not guarantee equal profit growth, and accounting profit does not imply an identical cash inflow. The income statement, balance sheet and cash-flow statement each add information.

In a fictional example, revenue rises from 100 to 120 while defined costs rise from 90 to 115. The simplified difference falls from ten to five. The example is not IndiGo's result. It shows why a traffic or sales headline cannot replace a complete financial comparison.

Read exceptional and other material items in the actual notes rather than removing inconvenient entries without explanation. If an adjusted measure is used, identify its method and source. A number becomes clearer when its definition is stated, not when the qualifications disappear.

## Share price is not an airline ticket price

A ticket price concerns a particular journey and fare conditions. A share quote concerns ownership in a listed company under a market record. A cheap ticket does not establish that the shares are undervalued, and a rising share price does not determine the fare a traveller will pay for a specific route.

The distinction also prevents misleading search pages. An article should not put a booking offer beside a stock figure and imply a direct equivalence. If discussing the company, use financial records; if discussing a journey, use the actual flight and fare terms. The two tasks need different evidence.

This guide does not give a target or a transaction instruction. It supplies an educational framework for understanding the business behind the search. Market expectations remain uncertain, and a single operating measure should not be turned into a guaranteed future outcome.

## Build a simple airline-metrics worksheet

Create separate columns for seats, distance, paying passengers, revenue and defined costs. Use fictional numbers first to test available-seat-kilometre, passenger-kilometre and ratio calculations. Label every unit so that a passenger count cannot accidentally be added to a currency amount.

Then add source, period and scope fields for any real data. Keep calculated ratios separate from the original inputs, and retain a note when a metric excludes a component. Another reader should be able to reproduce the result without guessing what a heading means.

This is a useful programming project as well as a spreadsheet exercise. Validate that denominators are present and appropriate, and do not replace missing information with zero. A small accurate tool is more valuable than a large dashboard built from mismatched periods and unlabeled ratios.
