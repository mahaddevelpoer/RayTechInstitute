Bonus shares are additional shares issued to eligible existing shareholders in a stated proportion. The ratio describes how many new shares relate to the shares already held. A larger share count does not, by itself, mean that a shareholder's investment has instantly gained the same percentage in economic value. Understanding the action requires separating units, price, ownership and the company's actual business performance.

This guide was checked on 5 November 2026 using SEBI investor-education material. It explains ratios and record reading through invented examples. It does not announce a company's bonus issue, establish an individual's entitlement or recommend buying a security. For a real action, use the company's official disclosure and current applicable information.

## What does a bonus-share ratio mean?

Read the ratio together with its written explanation. In a bonus issue described as two new shares for every one already held, an eligible holder's total becomes three units for each original unit. The two identifies the additional shares, not the final total. This is a common place for arithmetic mistakes.

Write the sentence before performing a calculation. For an invented example, a person holds forty shares and the stated action gives one additional share for every two held. The additional quantity is twenty, so the new total is sixty. The example assumes eligibility and a straightforward whole-number result only for teaching purposes.

Keep the original holding, additional quantity and final total in separate columns. If all three numbers are labelled, another learner can check the reasoning easily. A bare statement that the holding becomes one hundred and fifty percent can be confusing unless the reference quantity and meaning are made explicit.

## How can I calculate the additional shares?

For a simple ratio of a additional shares for every b existing shares, the illustrative calculation is existing quantity multiplied by a, divided by b. Add that result to the original quantity to obtain the hypothetical new total. Read the company's actual terms for any issue involving fractions or other conditions.

Suppose an invented holder has ninety shares and the example ratio is two for three. The additional quantity is ninety multiplied by two, divided by three, which is sixty. The total becomes one hundred and fifty. These numbers are classroom examples, not an announcement or an instruction about a current company.

Reverse the exercise to check understanding. Given an original quantity and a final total, calculate the additional quantity first, then examine its relationship to the original. This helps students see why a ratio should be interpreted rather than memorised as a vague promise of free money.

## Why does a bigger share count not automatically create wealth?

A company's economic value is not created merely by dividing an unchanged claim into more units. A bonus issue changes the number of shares through its stated mechanism. The price and other per-share quantities must be interpreted in relation to the changed count, rather than assuming that all old per-share figures remain unchanged.

Take an invented holding of fifty shares at a reference price of 200 units. Its illustrative value is 10,000 units. Under a simplified one-for-one example with an unchanged combined economic value, one hundred shares at a theoretical reference price of 100 units also represent 10,000. The example isolates the arithmetic; actual market prices can move for other reasons.

The phrase theoretical reference price matters. A classroom calculation is not a guarantee of the price available in the market or the value of an individual's account at a particular moment. Keep the calculation's assumptions visible instead of presenting the example as a prediction.

## How does proportional ownership work?

Ownership percentage compares a holder's shares with the relevant total share count. If both increase in the same proportion under a simplified example, the percentage can remain unchanged. The count of units and the fraction of the company are therefore different quantities that should be recorded separately.

Imagine a fictional company with 1,000 shares, of which a learner's example holder owns ten. The illustrative percentage is one percent. If a proportional action doubles both quantities, twenty out of 2,000 is still one percent. No real company or eligibility arrangement is implied by this exercise.

Ask what must be true for that conclusion to hold. The example assumes a consistent proportional change in the relevant quantities. Learning to state assumptions is important: the mathematical relationship can be correct while a real transaction requires additional information before the relationship can be applied confidently.

## How are bonus shares and a stock split different?

SEBI's educational material distinguishes a bonus issue from a split. A bonus issue provides additional shares under its stated ratio, while a split changes the denomination and number of existing units through a different mechanism. Face value and the accounting treatment should not be assumed identical simply because both can change a displayed share count.

Make a comparison table with action, what changes and which document explains it. Avoid writing more shares in every column and treating that as a complete explanation. The visible numerical result is only one part of understanding the corporate action and its supporting record.

For an actual company, use the official announcement's terminology. If the disclosure describes a split, do not relabel it as a bonus because the count increases. Precise language helps readers find the relevant rules and makes enquiries easier to understand when several different corporate actions are discussed at the same time.

## How is a dividend different from a bonus issue?

A dividend and a bonus issue are different corporate actions. SEBI's material discusses dividends as distributions and explains bonus shares separately. A statement about receiving additional units should not be rewritten as though it were automatically a cash payment into a bank account.

In a classroom exercise, give each example an action label before calculating anything. One example may describe additional shares; another may describe a stated cash amount per share. The arithmetic uses different quantities. Mixing the labels can produce a plausible-looking total while answering a different question from the one asked.

Do not infer future distributions from a bonus announcement alone. A larger number of units does not establish a future dividend rate, company profit or market return. Those questions require their own evidence and should not be answered by extending a simple share-count calculation beyond its assumptions.

## Which dates should I read in an announcement?

Identify the dates actually stated in the company's official documents and understand their individual purposes. An announcement date, a record date and other operational dates are not automatically interchangeable. Use the applicable current information rather than assuming that a historical example establishes today's eligibility or processing schedule.

Create a timeline with one row for each stated event and its source. If an important date is not established, mark it as unresolved. Do not invent the missing date by counting a familiar number of days from another event unless the relevant official instruction explicitly provides that relationship.

For a personal entitlement question, use the official record and appropriate intermediary information. A general article cannot establish a person's eligibility solely from a screenshot showing a recent purchase. The relevant details and applicable arrangements must be considered through the proper process.

## Why can a broker display look confusing around an action?

Different information fields may update according to their own processes. A displayed quantity, a reference price and a statement entry should be read with their labels and dates. A temporary difference on a screen is a reason to investigate the records, not proof of either an automatic profit or misconduct.

Keep the original statement and the official corporate-action disclosure. Note what you expected, what appeared and when you checked. A specific enquiry about a missing entry is easier to resolve than a message saying only that the whole account is wrong.

Do not change your own records to force them to match an assumption. Preserve the evidence and use the appropriate official support route. Where necessary, follow the issuer's or intermediary's documented grievance process. Avoid sending account credentials or unredacted financial records to strangers offering an instant correction through private messages.

## What should I check in a company disclosure?

Read the company name, action type, ratio, stated conditions and relevant dates. Check that the document concerns the security and event you are investigating. A widely circulated image can belong to another company or an older action, even when the wording resembles a current discussion.

Keep the source document distinct from commentary about it. A news headline can help identify a topic, but the company's actual disclosure is the reference for the action it announces. If commentary and the document appear inconsistent, inspect the precise wording before repeating the stronger claim.

For a learning project, summarise only the information supported by the record. Add a separate section for questions that remain unanswered. This produces a clearer explanation than filling every blank with a guess, and it makes the boundary between a documented fact and an interpretation visible to the reader.

## How can I avoid misleading bonus-share promotions?

Be cautious about messages that turn an increase in units into a guaranteed return. Ask what happens to the reference price, ownership fraction and assumptions in the calculation. If those questions are omitted, the promotion may be presenting only the part of the arithmetic that looks exciting.

Do not share payment credentials or verification codes with someone claiming to release bonus shares on your behalf. Use the official company, registrar or intermediary route appropriate to the issue. A familiar logo in a private message does not establish authority over a person's financial account.

Keep learning separate from a decision to purchase a security. Understanding a corporate action does not establish whether a company or product suits an individual. This article explains records and mathematics; it does not evaluate a person's objectives, financial circumstances or tolerance for risk.

## What is a useful practice worksheet?

Use invented cases with columns for original shares, stated ratio, additional shares and final quantity. Include a written explanation of the ratio before the calculation. Begin with whole-number examples so students can understand the relationship before exploring why real disclosures need to address more complicated conditions.

Add a separate theoretical-value exercise with clearly stated assumptions. Ask students to calculate the combined value before and after a proportional change in units. Then ask which assumptions would need checking before using the model to describe a real account or a live market price.

Finally, separate calculation from interpretation. A correct answer to how many shares does not answer whether the investment gained value or whether the holder is eligible. These distinctions are the most useful outcome of the worksheet because they prevent arithmetic confidence from becoming a substitute for reading the relevant evidence.

For an invented ratio exercise that produces a fractional result, show the fraction explicitly. Do not silently round it and present the rounded figure as a real entitlement. An actual action's treatment of fractions must come from its terms and official process, while a classroom worksheet can simply explain why the arithmetic created a non-whole number.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does one-for-one mean the final quantity is unchanged?

In an example stated as one additional share for each existing share, the additional quantity equals the original quantity and the final total doubles. Always read the written meaning of the ratio. Do not confuse the number of additional shares with the final total after the action.

### Does a bonus issue guarantee a higher investment value?

No such conclusion follows from share count alone. Read the change in units alongside the theoretical reference adjustment, the actual market information and the conditions of the action. A simple classroom example should not be presented as a promise of a particular price or financial return.
