## Why does the order of the team names matter?

Football scorecards commonly put the home team first. Elche 0–1 Valencia therefore gives Elche zero and Valencia one. Reversing the names without reversing the numbers would change the apparent winner. A clear card should label each team visibly rather than requiring the reader to infer the order from a badge or colour.

The same result can be expressed as “Valencia won 1–0 at Elche,” because that sentence explicitly identifies the winner and location. The two forms are compatible. Trouble begins when a sentence changes perspective but leaves an unlabeled score beside it, making readers unsure whose number comes first.

For an original interface exercise, write both forms and ask another person to identify the home side, away side and winner. If the answers differ, improve the labels. This is an information-design problem, not a reason to add more decorative icons around an already ambiguous number.

## What is a clean sheet?

A clean sheet means that the team did not concede a goal in the relevant match or recorded appearance context. Valencia's team result here contains zero goals conceded. A team clean sheet does not automatically settle every individual goalkeeper statistic if substitutions or different record conventions require additional information.

For an original example, a fictional side wins 2–0. It has a team clean sheet and a two-goal margin. A 2–1 win has the same winning team and the same number of goals scored, but no clean sheet. The additional conceded goal changes one description even though the three-point outcome remains a win.

Do not describe a clean sheet as proof that no danger existed. Opponents may have had attempts, and defenders may have made important interventions. The result says no goal was conceded; a fuller performance explanation needs evidence of what happened before the final whistle.

## How do corners differ from goals?

A corner is a restart opportunity, not a goal or a fixed fraction of a goal. Elche's official match page lists six corners for Elche and two for Valencia. Those counts show the recorded corner distribution. They do not change the final 0–1 score or prove that the side with more corners should have been awarded the win.

The recorded total is eight corners, with Elche taking six of them. Six divided by eight is seventy-five percent of the listed corners. Label that percentage precisely. It is not possession, share of all attacks, probability of winning or a measure of which team deserved the result.

This is a useful example of why a correct calculation can still be misused. The numerator and denominator support a corner-share statement. They do not support an unrelated conclusion about total dominance. Keep the calculation attached to the event category from which it was derived.

## Can more opportunities coexist with a defeat?

Yes. Football results depend on goals, while attempts, corners and other recorded events describe different parts of play. A team can record more of one opportunity type and still score fewer goals. That does not make the event count false or the result contradictory; the measures simply answer different questions.

Imagine an original fictional match with seven corners for one team and one for the other, ending 0–1. The corner count does not explain the quality of each delivery or the later shot. To assess that, a viewer needs the actual sequence and an appropriate definition of chance quality.

Avoid writing “more corners means better finishing.” Corners are not a denominator for a general shot-conversion rate. A chance may arise from open play, a free kick or another situation. Good analysis names the specific event being counted rather than converting every available statistic into the same broad conclusion.

## What is shot conversion, and what data is missing?

A simple shot-conversion rate divides goals by shots within a clearly defined sample. The definition of a shot and the provider's categories matter. ## How can students compare two match reports?

Start with shared identifiers: teams, date, competition and final score. Then separate facts from perspective. The winning club may emphasise the decisive goal and resilience, while the losing club may emphasise missed opportunities. Different emphasis can coexist with agreement on the underlying record.

For an original exercise, write two short summaries of a fictional 0–1 match from opposite perspectives. Underline every factual statement and circle every judgement. Ask whether a judgement has an example behind it. This teaches critical reading without requiring students to treat a club's voice as either perfectly neutral or automatically untrustworthy.
