## FBR electronic scrutiny rule 38B: what the official 2026 notification says

Pakistan's Federal Board of Revenue has added rule 38B to the Income Tax Rules, 2002. The official notification is S.R.O. 2098(I)/2026, dated 10 November 2026. It sets out a process in which an automated system can examine an income tax return, identify possible factual or legal mistakes and send an advance intimation through IRIS. The notice is intended to give the taxpayer an opportunity to explain, correct or otherwise address an issue before legal or penal action. A software flag is therefore the beginning of a review process, not proof that the taxpayer committed an offence.

The rule is of direct interest to people who file returns in Pakistan, accountants and students of tax administration. It is also easy to misdescribe. Some headlines call it an 'AI tax audit', but the rule itself refers to an automated system and cross-matching of available data. It does not publish the model, code, thresholds or error rate. It does not say that every flagged return will receive a penalty. The legal text is a short procedural rule; this explainer follows its nine numbered parts and avoids assigning powers to the software that the notification does not give it.

The [official FBR PDF](https://download1.fbr.gov.pk/SROs/202611111411117855SRO2098of2026.pdf) is the primary source. The notification says draft changes were previously published through S.R.O. 1690(I)/2026 on 6 October. That distinction matters: the October document was part of the rule-making sequence; S.R.O. 2098(I)/2026 is the November notification setting out the inserted text. A search result that finds the draft first should not assume it is the final wording.

### What 'electronic scrutiny' means here

Sub-rule 38B(1) says an automated system, including CRM, will process income returns under the referenced provision of the ordinance. Sub-rule 38B(8) refers to automated scrutiny, analysis and cross-matching of other available data. In ordinary language, the system compares information and identifies issues it believes require attention. The rule does not say that a discrepancy is always deliberate, or that the data sources are always complete. It establishes a way to surface possible issues for review.

An automated comparison can be useful when information in one record does not line up with information in another. There can also be harmless explanations: a timing difference, a corrected entry, an incomplete third-party record or a misunderstanding of how a field was reported. The rule's advance-intimation procedure exists so that an affected person can respond before further action. Calling every system output 'tax fraud' would skip that procedural step and mislead readers.

The notification does not provide a public list of every check the system will run. It also does not say that a human officer is absent. In fact, later sub-rules explicitly require communication with the officer who has jurisdiction and require that officer to analyse the response. This is an automated detection and electronic communication process with a human decision stage described in the rule.

### How an issue is communicated

Sub-rule 38B(2) provides for online advice or an advance intimation through IRIS. It says the message can point out factual or legal mistakes or discrepancies and allow the taxpayer to clarify, rectify or take another corrective action. Sub-rule 38B(3) says a jurisdictional Officer of Inland Revenue may also send the same system-generated advance intimation. The channel and sender details matter: the rule describes an IRIS workflow, not a demand from an unknown messaging account or a request to transfer money to a personal number.

The word 'advance' is important. It means the rule contemplates communication before legal or penal action. It does not guarantee that an issue will disappear merely because a response is filed, and it does not remove existing rights or obligations under the ordinance. It tells the reader the order in this specific procedure: detection, intimation, time for response, possible reminder, officer review and any action required under other law.

An intimation should identify the issue and state a response period. The rule does not prescribe one universal form of reply for every type of discrepancy. A factual typo, a missing document and a legal disagreement are different situations. A taxpayer who receives a real notice can read its exact contents, preserve a copy and consider professional help when the issue is complex. An article cannot determine the correct response for a particular return without seeing its facts and applicable law.

### The minimum seven-day response period

Sub-rule 38B(4) requires the intimation to specify a period for response that is not less than seven days. This is a minimum in the rule, not a promise that every notice will give exactly seven days. The deadline on an actual IRIS notice is the one the recipient needs to check. It would be misleading to convert the minimum into a fixed nationwide due date or to count from the article's publication date.

If no response is received in the stated period, sub-rule 38B(5) provides for a reminder. The reminder is to give a period of not less than seven days. The notification does not say that a reminder wipes out every earlier obligation or that silence after it is harmless. It says the reminder is part of the procedure after no response. The later officer review may consider the response, or the absence of one, under the ordinance and rules.

For a reader, the practical lesson is to treat the individual notice as a document with its own date and terms. The notification explains the minimum procedural framework; it is not a substitute for checking the taxpayer's IRIS account. Screenshots shared on social media can be cropped or outdated. The most reliable route to determine whether a notice is genuine is to access the official IRIS service directly through the FBR domain, rather than following an unsolicited link.

### The officer's role after a response

Sub-rule 38B(6) says the identified issues, the intimation and any response are communicated to the Officer of Inland Revenue with jurisdiction over the taxpayer. Sub-rule 38B(7) calls for intimations, responses and actions to be recorded in the system dashboard. These record-keeping provisions matter because they establish a traceable sequence rather than an invisible software judgment. The rule does not make the dashboard public, and this article does not suggest outsiders can see a taxpayer's record.

Sub-rule 38B(9) states that the jurisdictional officer analyses the taxpayer's response or the lack of one and takes appropriate action, if required, under relevant provisions of the Income Tax Ordinance and rules. The phrase 'if required' means the next step is conditional. It would be inaccurate to say that the algorithm automatically levies a penalty as soon as it detects a mismatch. The text places the later assessment of the response with an officer and refers to other legal provisions for any action.

The notification also mentions a Change Request Form in sub-rule 38B(8) for the implementation of the system. That is part of how FBR describes operating the process. It should not be presented as a public form that every taxpayer must fill in to answer a notice; the official text does not say that. Taxpayers should use the response instructions on an actual IRIS intimation.

### The difference between a flag, a notice and a decision

The three stages deserve separate names. A flag is an issue detected by automated analysis. A notice or advance intimation is the communication to a taxpayer through the procedure described in rule 38B. A decision or other legal action comes only after the relevant review and under the applicable law. One stage does not automatically prove the next. This distinction is vital when a short headline says that FBR 'found errors' in returns: the notification establishes a method for pointing out possible discrepancies and seeking clarification.

Suppose two records seem inconsistent. The system may identify a question, and the intimation should specify what needs attention. The taxpayer may have a valid explanation or may need to correct an error. The officer then considers the information available. This is a hypothetical illustration of the sequence, not a claim about any named taxpayer or a guarantee of a particular outcome. The precise legal consequences depend on the return, the notice and the ordinance.

Likewise, the rule does not publish an accuracy figure for the automated system. No article should claim that the model is infallible or that it wrongly flags a set percentage of people without evidence. The right question is whether the notice clearly describes the discrepancy and whether the person can use the stated response route. Transparent handling of mistakes is essential when software is used in an administrative process.

### How to recognise the official route and avoid fraud

FBR's own IRIS guidance identifies its online service on the `fbr.gov.pk` domain. FBR also warns that fraudulent SMS messages can ask people to call a number and reveal passwords, PINs or banking details. The new rule's reference to an IRIS intimation does not make every tax-themed text message genuine. Someone who receives a message should avoid entering credentials into a link from an unknown sender and should check the official service independently.

This point is especially important because a scam can copy the words 'electronic scrutiny' or 'rule 38B' from a public notification. A correct legal citation inside a message does not authenticate the sender. FBR's fraud warning says it does not seek banking credentials or PINs by SMS for a refund or other account activity. The notification itself concerns identifying discrepancies in returns, not asking for passwords. These sources together give a clear boundary without claiming that every legitimate communication looks identical.

The official PDF can be opened from FBR's site, and the rule number can be checked against it. An article or social post should link to the source document rather than reproduce a partial image with no date. The document's heading, SRO number and clauses allow a reader to distinguish the final notification from commentary about it. If an actual IRIS message is unclear, qualified tax advice may be appropriate; publishing personal tax documents in a public comment thread is not a safe way to seek help.

### Questions taxpayers and students may have

#### Does rule 38B apply to every tax return automatically?

The notification establishes an automated process for returns under the cited section. It does not say every filer will receive an intimation. Receipt depends on whether the system identifies an issue and sends one through the described process.

#### Is a discrepancy notice a penalty?

No. The rule describes an advance intimation intended to allow clarification or correction before legal or penal action. The later officer review and any action required under other law are separate stages.

#### How much time does a person have to answer?

The notice must specify a period of at least seven days. If there is no response, the rule provides for a reminder allowing at least seven days. The actual dates on the recipient's notice matter.

#### Can a human officer send the system-generated intimation?

Yes. Sub-rule 38B(3) allows the jurisdictional Officer of Inland Revenue to send the same system-generated advance intimation. The officer also has a later review role under sub-rule 38B(9).

#### Does the rule say AI makes the final decision?

No. It refers to an automated system and data cross-matching, while also assigning analysis of the response to the jurisdictional officer. The notification does not disclose a particular AI model.

#### Where is the official text?

FBR's PDF for S.R.O. 2098(I)/2026 is linked in the sources below. It is the document to check for the exact wording of rule 38B and its nine sub-rules.

#### What should a person do with a suspicious SMS?

FBR warns against disclosing PINs, passwords or banking details to callers or links in fraudulent tax messages. Independently open the official FBR or IRIS site and check the account rather than relying on the SMS link.

### Why this change matters beyond one filing season

Rule 38B is an example of public administration using software to surface questions at scale. Its value depends on data quality, clear notices, secure communication, meaningful response time and a review process that can correct errors. The official text explicitly includes response and officer-review steps. Those safeguards should be visible in how the system operates, and taxpayers need trustworthy ways to distinguish genuine notices from imitations.

For students of computer science, the rule also illustrates a broader point: an automated output is evidence to evaluate, not a complete explanation. Software can compare fields and identify patterns, but a person may have context the stored data lack. A sound process records the flag, communicates it, allows a response and assigns an accountable reviewer. That observation follows the rule's sequence; it is not a claim that every implementation detail has already been independently audited.

For taxpayers, the most reliable starting place is the official notification and the actual IRIS message, if one arrives. General articles can explain the rule, but an individual return may raise questions of fact and law that require a qualified adviser. The notification creates a procedure for communication and response. It should be read as that procedure, not as an automatic conviction, a blanket amnesty or a prediction about the outcome of any particular case.
