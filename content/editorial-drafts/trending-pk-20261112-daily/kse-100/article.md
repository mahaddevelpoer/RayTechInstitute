## KSE-100: identify the trading session before reading the number

Reports published on 12 November 2026 describe the KSE-100's Wednesday, 11 November close at 179,083.80 points, down 251.44 points from 179,335.24. The reported percentage decline is 0.14%. The publication date and the trading-session date are different: this figure must not be labelled the completed 12 November close.

That correction is important for anyone arriving from a search for the index “today.” A news article can be current while reporting the previous completed session. A static explanation should preserve that date rather than make an older number look like a live market quote.

This guide explains the dated report and the arithmetic behind index moves. It provides general financial education, not a recommendation about a security, a personalised portfolio decision or a forecast of the next session.

## The reported session in one table

| Measure | Reported value |
| --- | --- |
| Trading session | Wednesday, 11 November 2026 |
| Closing index level | 179,083.80 |
| Previous close | 179,335.24 |
| Change | Minus 251.44 points |
| Rounded percentage change | Minus 0.14% |

The values above are a historical checkpoint. For the current market, use the official PSX data portal and read its displayed timestamp and session status. A delayed quote, an intraday value and a completed close answer different questions.

Avoid relying on a page that changes the heading date automatically while leaving the underlying value unchanged. A useful quote identifies what it measures, when it was measured and which source supplied it. Without those details, an exact-looking number can still mislead.

## Index points are not rupees

An index level is a benchmark value calculated under a methodology. It is not the price of one share, the amount invested by every participant or a rupee balance belonging to an individual. A 251-point movement does not mean every investor gained or lost Rs251.

The KSE-100 methodology brochure describes a benchmark with a November 1991 base of 1,000 points and a free-float approach. The current level is meaningful through its relationship with earlier index levels and the rules behind it, not as a standalone cash amount.

When discussing the market with a new learner, keep the unit beside the number. Write “index points” for the level and “rupees” for a share price or portfolio value. This prevents an understandable but consequential confusion between a market indicator and a person's money.

## Reproduce the percentage calculation

The percentage change uses the earlier level as its base. Divide the change of minus 251.44 by 179,335.24 and multiply by 100. The result is approximately minus 0.1402%, which rounds to minus 0.14%.

Do not divide by the new close simply because it is the larger or more prominent number in a headline. That would answer a slightly different calculation. A spreadsheet should show the previous close, new close and formula in separate cells so that another reader can reproduce the result.

Rounding also matters. A published percentage may use two decimal places while the underlying calculation contains more. A small difference at additional decimal places is not necessarily an error. Compare the method and precision before deciding that two summaries contradict one another.

## Why the same point change can mean different things

An illustrative 100-point move from an index level of 1,000 equals 10%. The same 100-point move from 10,000 equals 1%. The point count is identical, but its scale relative to the starting value differs.

This is why a long-term comparison of “record point moves” needs context. A larger index level can make a large point movement less exceptional in percentage terms. The percentage does not replace the point figure; it complements it.

These examples are fictional teaching values, not additional KSE-100 sessions. Keep that label when using them in a class or social post. Otherwise, an exercise intended to explain arithmetic can accidentally become a fabricated historical market report.

## What free float means

Free float concerns shares considered available for normal trading under the relevant methodology, rather than simply every share a company has issued. Shares held in particular locked or controlling categories may be treated differently. The official brochure is the reference for its definitions.

The practical idea is that an index can weight companies using a measure intended to reflect tradable market value. That differs from giving every company an equal vote or treating every issued share as equally available in the market.

This explanation does not substitute a simplified classroom formula for the exchange's complete calculation. Real index maintenance also involves defined treatment of changes and corporate actions. The point is to understand why the word “weighted” matters before interpreting the benchmark's movement.

## A simplified two-company example

Imagine two fictional companies with the same total market value. Company A has 100 shares priced at Rs10, giving Rs1,000. Company B has 200 shares priced at Rs5, also giving Rs1,000. Under a simple full-value comparison, they would begin with equal weight.

Now suppose only 50 of A's shares and all 200 of B's shares count in a simplified free-float exercise. The corresponding values are Rs500 and Rs1,000. A represents one third of the combined value and B represents two thirds.

If A rises 10% while B is unchanged, the simplified weighted change is about 3.33%, rather than the 5% obtained from equal weights. This is not a miniature reproduction of the full KSE-100 rulebook. It demonstrates how different weighting choices can produce different results from the same company-price movements.

## Why not every constituent has to fall

A weighted index can decline even when some companies rise. What matters is the combination of movements and weights, rather than a rule that every constituent must travel in the same direction.

Consider another fictional basket: weights of 50%, 30% and 20%, with returns of plus 2%, minus 1% and zero. The contributions are plus 1 percentage point, minus 0.3 percentage points and zero, producing a combined plus 0.7%. If the starting illustrative level were 1,000, that simplified move would take it to 1,007.

The example shows why a headline index and the count of rising or falling shares measure different things. Neither is inherently useless. They answer different questions and become more informative when kept separate.

## Market breadth, volume and turnover

Market breadth describes how widely gains or losses are spread across securities. Trading volume concerns the number of shares traded, while traded value concerns the money value of those transactions. An index level is another measure again.

A large volume in a low-priced security can differ substantially from the same volume in a high-priced security when measured in rupees. Therefore, “most active” needs a definition: by shares, value or another metric? Read the table heading rather than assuming all activity rankings are equivalent.

This guide does not add an unverified turnover or breadth figure to the 11 November report. A historical closing level can be discussed accurately without manufacturing the rest of a market dashboard.

## Total return and price return

The PSX methodology document describes the KSE-100's treatment of dividends and corporate actions. A benchmark designed to account for distributions is not the same thing as a chart that follows only an unadjusted price series.

For an ordinary comparison, imagine a fictional investment whose price falls from Rs100 to Rs95 while paying Rs5 in cash. Looking only at the final price omits the distribution. Whether and how a particular benchmark accounts for it depends on its defined methodology.

This does not mean every investor receives exactly the benchmark's result. Taxes, fees, timing and holdings can differ. It means a comparison must identify what each series includes before declaring one return higher than another.

## A changing index is not a fixed list forever

An index has rules for selecting and maintaining its constituents. A historical brochure or an old company list should not automatically be presented as the exact current composition. Consult the exchange's current notices for that purpose.

The distinction matters when explaining a past move. A company that belongs to the index now may not have had the same role in another period. Similarly, current weights should not be applied backwards without checking the appropriate historical information.

For learners, retain the date of any constituent list used in a calculation. That makes the exercise reproducible and prevents an updated list from silently changing the meaning of an earlier comparison.

## The index is not your personal portfolio

A person holding a few selected securities has a different mix from the benchmark. Their entry prices, quantities and transaction dates also differ. It is therefore possible for the index to rise while a particular portfolio falls, or for a portfolio to rise by a different percentage.

A fair performance comparison needs a matching period and an appropriate benchmark. Comparing one day's index movement with a portfolio's return over several months does not answer a clear question. Nor does comparing a gross benchmark return with a personal cash outcome without considering the relevant differences.

This article does not evaluate a reader's holdings or recommend a change. Its purpose is to show why a broad market indicator and an individual's result cannot be treated as the same number.

## Intraday values and completed closes

An intraday quote records a point during the session. A completed close identifies the end-of-session record. A news page written before the session finishes cannot establish the final close merely by using the word “today.”

Keep the timestamp and time zone if they are relevant to a comparison. A screenshot shared later may still be useful evidence of what a page displayed at that time, but it does not become a current quote because it appears in a new message.

For this report, the safe label is the 11 November close reported on 12 November. A later session's result requires a new source. That precision is more useful than an apparently fresh heading attached to yesterday's data.

## A market-reading worksheet

Create columns for session date, source, previous close, new close, point change and percentage change. Add a column for whether the value is intraday, delayed or final. Calculate the difference yourself and compare it with the published rounding.

Then keep separate notes for explanation and evidence. A report may attribute a movement to profit-taking or sentiment, but that interpretation is not the same kind of fact as the closing number. Retain the attribution rather than presenting one explanation as the sole proven cause.

This method gives students a practical introduction to financial information without encouraging an immediate trading decision. Understanding a number is a valuable task in its own right.

## Questions about the KSE-100 checkpoint

### Is 179,083.80 the 12 November closing value?

Not in the reports reviewed here. They were published on 12 November and describe the Wednesday, 11 November close. The trading date must remain attached to the figure. Check the official portal for a different session or a current quote.

### Does a 0.14% decline mean every stock fell 0.14%?

No. The benchmark combines movements under its weighting rules. Individual securities can move by different amounts and in different directions. The broad index does not assign the same return to every constituent or every person's holdings.

### Are index points a cash amount?

No. They describe a calculated benchmark level. A portfolio's rupee gain or loss needs its own holdings and prices. Confusing the units can make a correct market headline seem to promise a personal financial outcome that it does not establish.

### Does this article predict the next session?

No. It explains a dated record and how to read similar information. A historical movement does not guarantee a future return, and this guide offers no personalised investment recommendation.

## Keep the evidence and interpretation separate

A market report becomes clearer when the date, unit and calculation are visible. An exact-looking figure without those labels can be less useful than a modest explanation that shows its limits.

## Check the arithmetic before sharing a market graphic

Keep the index level, point change and percentage change together. A number copied without its label can turn a modest percentage movement into an exaggerated claim. If rounding produces a small difference, explain the precision used rather than altering a reported figure to make a graphic look tidier. The publication date and the trading-session date should remain visible on any summary you create.
