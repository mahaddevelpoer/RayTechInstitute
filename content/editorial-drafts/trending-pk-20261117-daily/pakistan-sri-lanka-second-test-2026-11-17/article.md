### Why is the second Test in Rawalpindi?

The PCB announced that the second Test would be relocated from Lahore to Rawalpindi because of prevailing weather conditions. Associated reporting described severe Lahore smog as the reason for the decision. Rawalpindi had already hosted the opening Test, so the shift meant consecutive Test matches at the same ground rather than a change to an unfamiliar stadium.

For spectators, venue information affects ticket searches, travel plans and weather expectations. For readers outside Pakistan, it also explains why some early fixtures and social posts can conflict with newer match listings. The most reliable order is to prefer the PCB's later press release, the PCB scorecard and the ICC's current match listing over an earlier schedule announcement.

The relocation does not alter the status of the match as a Test or its place in the series. It changes where it is played. It is therefore inaccurate to describe the game as cancelled or to say that Lahore hosted it simply because the original schedule did.

### What happened in the first Test?

The opening Test at Rawalpindi ended in a draw on 13 November. Pakistan declared their first innings at 434 for 9. Sri Lanka replied with 446, taking a 12-run first-innings lead. Dhananjaya de Silva made 167 and Kusal Mendis made 119 for Sri Lanka. Pakistan then reached 284 for 8 in their second innings and denied Sri Lanka a second chance to bat before the match ended.

Pakistan's Saud Shakeel was a major figure in that draw. The ICC report says he top-scored with 93 in Pakistan's first innings and then made 97 in the second. Saqib Khan took seven wickets on his Test debut and was named Player of the Match. Those figures give useful context for the second Test, but they should not be copied into the second match scorecard. Each innings, spell and dismissal belongs to its own fixture.

Because the first Test was drawn, neither team entered the second Test with a series lead. The result of this match will decide whether either side wins the two-Test series or whether it ends level. That makes the live score important, but it does not turn the first morning into a completed result.

### How the second Test began

Pakistan elected to field after winning the toss. The early PCB scorecard listed Sri Lanka's first six wickets falling at 6, 16, 25, 25, 36 and 85. That sequence shows a difficult start, including two wickets with the score on 25. The dismissed batters at the initial checkpoint included Pasindu Sooriyabandara, Dinesh Chandimal, Kamindu Mendis, Lahiru Udara, Dhananjaya de Silva and Kusal Mendis.

The order of wickets tells a reader more than a single score alone. A side can be 85 for 6 because of a collapse followed by a recovery, or because wickets fell evenly through a disciplined session. The scorecard's fall-of-wickets line helps distinguish those possibilities. Still, it does not establish what will happen next. Lower-order batting, a partnership, changing light and the pace of the pitch all matter in a Test match.

When a score page updates, the important fields are the total, wickets, overs, current batters and bowlers. The batting total cannot be evaluated properly without the number of wickets down, and the number of wickets cannot be evaluated without knowing how far the innings has progressed.

### How to follow the score without being misled

Live cricket pages can be delayed, cached or republished by several outlets. Use the PCB scorecard as the primary source for this match. The ICC match page is helpful for the fixture and competition context. If a social media post conflicts with the official scorecard, wait for the scorecard to update or compare the time of the post before sharing it.

Avoid reading a headline such as “Sri Lanka collapse” as a final result. In a Test, an innings can change after a lower-order partnership. Likewise, an early bowling success does not prove that the pitch will stay equally difficult later in the day. The same applies to projected scores: they are estimates, not facts.

For a quick check, verify five things: the date and session, the venue, current innings, runs and wickets, and whether the source labels the match live or complete. This prevents two frequent errors: importing a number from the first Test and reporting an in-progress score as a final score.

### A practical scorecard reading guide

The top line of a Test scorecard gives a snapshot, not the full story. “85/6” means 85 runs for the loss of six wickets. It does not identify the current partnership, the number of balls faced, extras, conditions, or the quality of the remaining batting. The overs figure helps with that context. Twenty-nine overs on the opening day is still early in a five-day match, even when six wickets have fallen.

The batting table shows which dismissals have occurred and whether a batter was out caught, bowled, leg before wicket, run out or stumped. Those labels should not be used to make unsupported claims about poor technique or an umpiring error. They only describe the recorded mode of dismissal. The bowling table records wickets, runs, overs and economy, but Test bowling is usually assessed over longer spells than a short-format match.

Extras also matter. Byes and leg byes add to the innings total without being charged to a bowler. Wides and no-balls add to the team total and to the bowler's conceded runs. A live article should therefore avoid reconstructing bowling figures from the batting score alone. The official tables are the correct source for precise individual figures.

The scorecard can also be corrected after a review or an administrative update. A report that names the exact time of its score check is more transparent than one that silently leaves an old live score on the page. This article does that deliberately. It gives the first-day 85/6 checkpoint as a dated search context and directs readers to the live scorecard for all later changes.
