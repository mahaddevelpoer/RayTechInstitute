Pakistan Women beat Scotland Women by nine wickets in the ICC Women's Emerging Nations Trophy on 22 November 2026. The Pakistan Cricket Board reports Scotland's innings as **124/5 in 20 overs** and Pakistan's successful reply as **128/1 in 17.1 overs**. Those three numbers make an excellent lesson in targets, winning margins and the difference between a cricket over and a decimal fraction.

This guide uses the completed match to explain what a scorecard establishes and what requires additional evidence. It does not turn one result into a prediction about a future match or an unverified claim about tournament qualification. The arithmetic below is our calculation from the identified score entries. Illustrative examples are labelled separately from the reported match figures.

## Why was the target 125 when Scotland scored 124?

In a straightforward unrevised chase, the second side needs to exceed the first side's completed total. The first-innings figure here was 124, so the ordinary target was **125**. Reaching 124 would only match that total; it would not put the chasing side ahead.

This distinction is useful whenever a scoreboard displays both a target and the opponent's score. The target is a requirement for the chase, while the first-innings score is a completed record. They are related but they are not interchangeable labels. A student who copies 124 into a target field can introduce an error before doing any calculation.

A safe worksheet can therefore begin with separate cells called First-innings runs and Target. For this match record, enter 124 in the first and 125 in the second. Do not make the formula universal without qualification: revised targets, penalties and competition-specific circumstances must be handled using the official match record. Here, use the identified ordinary target rather than inventing a rain adjustment.

An equally important distinction is between a target and a predicted total. A chase target is part of the match situation. A forecast is an estimate about something that has not happened. Confusing the two can make a speculative graphic look like an official requirement.

## How can a team finish on 128 while chasing 125?

A successful chase does not have to end at exactly the target number. The treatment of winning runs follows the applicable cricket laws and playing conditions. In particular, a boundary can take the final total beyond the minimum requirement. The number displayed at the end is the recorded innings total, not a value automatically clipped to the target.

The entries 125 as the target and 128 as the final total are therefore not, by themselves, contradictory. Their difference is **three runs**, but that subtraction is not the official winning margin in this successful chase. The match result was expressed in wickets, as explained in the next section.

Do not infer an exact final delivery from those totals alone. A full ball-by-ball record is needed to state precisely how the innings ended. A learner can explain that going beyond the target is possible without claiming to have watched a particular shot or inventing commentary.

This is a useful evidence exercise: write down what the totals demonstrate, then write down what they do not demonstrate. They show the recorded final runs and that the second innings exceeded the opponent's total. They do not independently reveal the delivery type, shot direction, field placement or sequence of events on the last ball.

## Why is the result described as nine wickets?

In this completed chase, Pakistan had lost one wicket when the winning requirement was achieved. The ordinary ten-wicket innings framework leaves **10 minus 1, or nine wickets**, and the official result records a nine-wicket victory. That describes the remaining wickets when the chase succeeded.

A beginner may instead subtract 124 from 128 and call it a four-run victory. That is not the reported result. The four-run difference between the two final totals is a true subtraction, but it answers a different numerical question. Arithmetic can be correct while the label attached to it is wrong.

Compare a clearly fictional example. Team A makes 150 and Team B finishes on 151/7 in a successful ordinary chase. The remaining-wickets calculation is ten minus seven, giving three. Now imagine another fictional match in which the side batting first defends its total. The relevant official margin is then expressed differently, so the same remaining-wickets shortcut should not be applied indiscriminately.

When writing a match summary, copy the result wording from the official scorecard and use your calculation as a check. Do not replace an official result with a homemade label simply because two totals can be subtracted.

## What does 17.1 overs mean in valid balls?

The entry **17.1** is cricket notation: 17 completed overs and one further valid delivery. With six valid deliveries in an over, the conversion is **17 multiplied by 6, plus 1, which equals 103 valid balls**. It does not mean 17.1 decimal overs.

A full 20-over allocation contains 120 valid balls under that ordinary six-ball convention. Subtracting 103 gives **17 valid balls unused**. This reconciles the recorded innings notation with the statement that the chase finished with 17 balls to spare.

The decimal equivalent for rate calculations is 103 divided by six, or approximately **17.1667 overs**. That is a different representation from the scoreboard string. Keeping the display value and the calculation value in separate fields prevents a common spreadsheet mistake.

For a fictional comparison, 12.5 means 12 completed overs plus five valid balls, giving 77 balls. It does not mean twelve and a half overs. After the next valid delivery, the notation advances to 13.0 rather than 12.6. Wides and no-balls add their applicable runs and consequences but do not become one of the six valid deliveries merely because a ball was physically delivered.

## How should the two innings' scoring rates be compared?

Using the reported totals, Scotland's simple scoring rate is **124 divided by 20, or 6.20 runs per over**. Pakistan's equivalent calculation uses valid balls: **128 multiplied by 6, divided by 103, or approximately 7.46 runs per over**.

A rate is a normalisation of runs by playing time measured here in valid deliveries. It helps compare innings that used different numbers of balls. It does not describe every phase of the innings. A final average can conceal slower opening overs, faster later overs or the effect of a particular partnership.

Nor does a higher overall rate automatically prove that every batter attacked in the same way. Shot selection, wickets, conditions and match requirements all matter. Use the rate as one summary measure, then inspect the appropriate underlying evidence for a tactical claim.

For a spreadsheet check, store runs and valid balls as numbers and calculate runs times six divided by balls. Label the output Runs per over and choose a suitable rounding rule, such as two decimal places. Do not divide by the text 17.1 and call the result an accurate over-based rate. Do not mix deliveries actually used with the original allocation without explaining the change of denominator.

## What can the two unbeaten half-centuries teach?

PCB reports Muneeba's 59 not out from 48 balls and Gull's 54 not out from 43. For a simple batting strike rate, divide runs by balls faced and multiply by 100. Our calculations give approximately **122.92** for 59 from 48 and **125.58** for 54 from 43.

These rates are close, but a numerical comparison alone is a limited description of the innings. It does not show which deliveries were faced, when boundaries occurred or how the batters responded to the match situation. A performance assessment should not pretend that one rounded statistic contains the whole contribution.

Not out also has a precise reporting role: the batter was not dismissed in that innings. It does not automatically mean that the batter faced every remaining delivery or that the entire innings consisted of one partnership. Distinguish the player's status from the team's result.

The two reported scores add to **113**. That sum is not automatically the partnership total, because a partnership measures team runs added between two particular wicket events or the end of an innings. It can include extras, and a batter may already have scored before the partnership began. Use a partnership entry or the full sequence to make that claim accurately.

## Why should batter totals and team totals be reconciled?

A scorecard has several ledgers: individual batting runs, extras, wickets and the team total. These are related records, rather than unrelated decorative statistics. Checking their relationships can expose a transcription error before it reaches a published article or school worksheet.

For a fictional innings, suppose three batters contribute 40, 35 and 20 and the extras entry is five. The sum is 100. If a copied team total says 110, the worksheet needs investigation. One should not invent an extra ten runs merely to force agreement; return to the original scorecard and identify the missing or mistyped entry.

The same discipline applies to a real completed match, but use the full record rather than an abbreviated report that may list only the leading performers. A short news summary need not contain every batter or every extras category. Missing detail in an extract does not establish that the original scorecard is inconsistent.

For students, this is also a lesson in data completeness. Mark a source as a brief score or a full scorecard. Record which fields are available. A calculation that requires every batter's contribution cannot be fully audited using a paragraph that names only the top two scorers.

## Does one victory establish the final Super Sixes position?

The result establishes the outcome of this match. It does not, on its own, establish every team's final tournament position, the complete qualification picture or the applicable tie-break calculation. Those questions require the competition's table and playing conditions at the relevant point in time.

A table can incorporate earlier results, stage-specific arrangements and rate-based tie-breakers. Do not reconstruct it from one match headline without checking how the competition handles those inputs. A familiar term such as Super Sixes should prompt a rules check rather than an assumption that every tournament uses the same carry-forward system.

Likewise, **innings run rate** and **tournament net run rate** are different measures. The two rates calculated above describe scoring in these particular innings. A net-run-rate figure needs the applicable aggregated rules and, in some circumstances, adjusted treatment of overs. Subtracting the two displayed averages is not a reliable substitute for reading the official standings.

The careful wording is that Pakistan won this fixture by nine wickets. A further claim about qualification should be supported separately, with a current table and an explanation of the relevant rule. Keeping result, arithmetic and tournament implication separate makes the report easier to verify.
