**Malaysia is a Southeast Asian federation with 13 states and three federal territories.** Its geography has two main parts, Peninsular Malaysia and East Malaysia, separated by the South China Sea. These distinctions are useful when reading a map, comparing a population table or identifying what a news report means by a Malaysian place name.

This guide uses Malaysia's official government portal and Department of Statistics Malaysia resources, checked on 23 November 2026. It explains geography and public-data reading rather than inventing a current population estimate, travel price or immigration requirement. The numerical exercises are explicitly fictional unless a source table is identified.

## Where are Peninsular Malaysia and East Malaysia?

The government portal places Peninsular Malaysia south of Thailand, with Singapore to its south. East Malaysia occupies the northern part of Borneo and includes Sabah and Sarawak, together with the federal territory of Labuan. Indonesia and Brunei are important neighbouring references when identifying that region on a map.

The two parts belong to one country, even though a small regional map may show only one of them. A map cropped around the peninsula therefore does not display Malaysia's entire geographic extent. Read the map title and frame before concluding that an omitted area belongs elsewhere.

An inset can show a distant area at a different scale. That makes the page convenient to print, but the distance between two boxes on the page is not a measurement of the real distance between the places. Each panel needs its own scale and geographic context.

For a notebook exercise, outline the two regions and label the intervening sea. Then identify which source establishes the country boundary and which panel supplies detail about a particular state. The aim is to understand the map's structure before memorising a collection of place names.

## How do the states and federal territories differ as categories?

The official government and statistics resources distinguish states from federal territories. Kuala Lumpur, Putrajaya and Labuan are federal territories; they should not be described as three additional states. Adding the two category counts gives sixteen state-or-territory units, not sixteen states.

Peninsular Malaysia contains eleven states and the federal territories of Kuala Lumpur and Putrajaya. East Malaysia contains Sabah, Sarawak and Labuan. This grouping explains why a region count, a state count and a state-or-territory count can all be correct while producing different numbers.

Write the category beside the value in a revision table. A column headed States should not quietly include federal territories. If a chart uses a combined category, its heading should say so. Clear headings matter as much as the arithmetic when a reader checks the total.

This is a classification exercise rather than a statement that every administrative function works identically in every unit. A geographic label and the legal powers of an institution are different questions. Consult the relevant official material before extending a map-based observation into a detailed governance claim.

## Which names belong in a basic state-reference list?

The state names shown in the government portal include Johor, Kedah, Kelantan, Melaka, Negeri Sembilan, Pahang, Pulau Pinang, Perak, Perlis, Sabah, Sarawak, Selangor and Terengganu. Keep this list distinct from the three federal territories named above.

A table can contain both an official name and an alternative name used in another source. For example, a spelling or translation difference should prompt a check of the record, rather than automatically creating a new geographic unit in a spreadsheet.

When compiling a learning sheet, assign one row to each unit and a second column to its category. Count the state rows independently from the territory rows. This simple check catches a common transcription error: including a familiar city name as though it were another state.

Do not attach an unverified current officeholder, population or administrative ranking merely to make every row appear complete. A reference list can serve its purpose with verified names and categories. Extra columns require their own evidence and reference dates.

## Why should a city, state and country occupy separate fields?

A city name identifies a different geographic level from the country that contains it. A report about a city can be relevant to national discussion without describing conditions throughout Malaysia. The location named in the headline should therefore remain visible when the story is summarised.

Consider a fictional observation recorded at one urban transport stop. Its location field should identify that site or city. Labelling the measurement simply Malaysia would remove information about where it was obtained and could encourage an unsupported national interpretation.

The same issue appears in photographs. An image of a landmark establishes what the photograph depicts and, if properly documented, where it was taken. It does not establish an average condition across every district or a current measurement of the national economy.

For a geography assignment, use fields for Country, State or territory, Local area and Exact site if available. Leave a field unknown when the source does not identify it. Precision should follow the evidence, rather than be invented to make a form look finished.

## How is a statistical boundary different from a familiar place name?

A dataset has a defined unit of observation. The word district in one table may refer to an administrative district, while another publication may use a constituency or a census-operation unit. Similar place labels do not guarantee that the areas have identical boundaries.

DOSM's census technical notes describe administrative districts and smaller units used for census operations. Those notes are important because a statistical table can count reporting units that are not the same thing as the country's states or its familiar cities.

When comparing tables, record the geographic level exactly as the publisher describes it. A total for a constituency should not be treated as the population of a whole state merely because the constituency lies within that state. The relationship between levels needs to be established.

A useful worksheet asks students to sort labels into country, state or territory, administrative district and statistical unit. Where a source uses a specialised term, add its definition beside the label. The exercise turns a map into a structured description rather than an unconnected list of names.

## What does the OpenDOSM DUN population dataset cover?

OpenDOSM provides a population dataset at state legislative assembly constituency, or DUN, level. Its documentation explains that federal territories are absent because they do not have state legislative assemblies. A missing territory row in that dataset is therefore a scope decision, not evidence that the territory has no people.

This example illustrates why a dataset's description must be read before its totals are interpreted. A table designed around one institutional geography cannot automatically answer every question about the whole country's administrative units.

The documentation also distinguishes a DUN from a parliamentary constituency. A student joining two tables should use the provided identifiers and definitions rather than assume that all political or administrative boundaries represent the same unit.

For a source sheet, record Dataset purpose, Geographic unit, Included areas and Excluded areas. Those four fields can explain an apparent omission before anyone starts guessing why a number is absent. They are especially useful when an attractive dashboard displays only a short label above a large number.

## Why does a population unit marked thousands matter?

The OpenDOSM documentation identifies its population values as thousands of people. A figure presented in that unit needs conversion before it is compared with a table expressed in individual persons. The decimal point does not make the number an error; the unit determines its interpretation.

In a fictional example, a population value of **1.25 thousand people means 1,250 people**. Multiplying by 1,000 performs the conversion. It does not mean that the location has one and a quarter residents, and it should not be rounded to one person before conversion.

Now imagine another fictional table recording 1,250 persons. The two values represent the same count under those stated units. A comparison that treats 1.25 and 1,250 as directly competing values would manufacture a thousandfold discrepancy.

Keep the unit in the spreadsheet heading and in any chart label. If you convert the numbers, change the label at the same time and preserve a note about the conversion. An unlabeled transformed column is difficult for another student to check, even when the arithmetic itself is correct.

## How should the observation year be separated from the update date?

A dataset can be updated after the period its values describe. The date of a website update is therefore not automatically the year of the population observation. A current-looking page can legitimately provide historical data if that is its stated purpose.

OpenDOSM's DUN documentation describes annual observations and explains the date field's structure. Read that field together with the available years. Do not treat a display date such as January 1 as proof that a complete census was physically conducted on that exact day.

Create separate columns for Reference year, Publication or update date and Date accessed. The first identifies the period measured, the second identifies the source's release history, and the third records when you consulted it. Combining them into a single date field loses information.

When a later edition appears, preserve the edition used in your calculation. A classmate comparing the same table after an update may encounter a revised value. A source reference should make that difference explainable instead of turning it into an argument about who copied the number correctly.

## How can a population rate tell a different story from a total?

Counts describe the size of a quantity, while rates relate it to a denominator. A larger area can have more facilities in total but fewer facilities per resident. Neither statement is automatically wrong; they answer different questions.

Use two fictional areas. Area A has 12 libraries for 60,000 residents; Area B has 20 for 200,000. Per 10,000 residents, A has **12 ÷ 60,000 × 10,000 = 2**, while B has **20 ÷ 200,000 × 10,000 = 1**. B has the larger count, but A has the larger rate in this invented dataset.

Those numbers are not Malaysian library statistics. They demonstrate why the denominator must be stated before a comparison is interpreted. A real investigation would require verified facility definitions, reference years and compatible geographic boundaries.

The rate alone also does not establish service quality, access or opening hours. A calculation can help frame a question without answering every aspect of it. Explain what the indicator measures and what further evidence would be needed before making a broader judgement.

## What should a map legend and scale be used for?

A legend explains the meaning assigned to symbols and colours. A shaded area might represent a category, a count or a range of values. Read the legend before treating a darker shade as a universal sign of better or worse conditions.

For a fictional choropleth map, a colour could indicate population density rather than total population. A geographically small area might have a darker shade while containing fewer people overall than a larger area. The legend and unit establish which interpretation is supported.

A scale helps translate a distance on the map into an approximate real-world distance under the map's stated conditions. It does not establish travel duration, road availability or a ticket price. Those require different information and can change over time.

In a class exercise, describe the map using its title, legend, scale, source and reference date. Then write one conclusion the map supports and one tempting conclusion it does not support. This habit improves both geographic understanding and the accuracy of captions used in a report.

## How can a student compare Malaysia with Pakistan carefully?

Begin with the exact quantity being compared. A number of states is not the same measure as a number of provinces, and an administrative label does not automatically describe an equivalent population size or area. Explain the categories rather than assuming identical arrangements.

Our [province guide](/blogs/what-is-a-province-pakistan-federation-districts-guide/) provides a separate introduction to Pakistan's geographic categories. Use each country's official sources for its own structure, then state which feature your comparison concerns.

For population comparisons, align the reference years, units and geographic coverage. If one table reports thousands and another reports persons, convert the units explicitly. If the observation years differ, disclose that difference rather than presenting the values as simultaneous measurements.

A short comparison can remain useful without a league table. Students can explain how the two sources organise information, identify which units are included and describe one calculation that would be valid under matched conditions. That teaches a transferable method while preserving each country's particular geography.
