## Why games played matter

Arsenal had played eight matches in that table, while City and Brighton had played nine. A points comparison that hides the played column loses an important part of the context.

Having a game in hand is an opportunity, not points already earned. A team still needs to play the fixture, and its eventual result determines what is added. Do not award three imaginary points simply because an additional game is available.

The same care works in reverse. A side with more points after playing more fixtures is not automatically certain to finish above a rival. The current table records completed results; it does not settle all the remaining matches.

For a school exercise, copy the snapshot into a spreadsheet and add a separate column labelled “hypothetical next result.” Keep that column visibly separate from the official points. This prevents an imagined future scenario from quietly becoming part of the reported table.

## A shot is an attempt, not a goal

The match-data provider's report records City taking 21 shots, six on target, compared with Brighton's seven attempts and three on target. Brighton still won 1–0. Those figures show the difference between counting attacking attempts and counting the goals that decide a match.

Dividing six by 21 gives approximately 28.6% for City's share of attempts recorded on target. Brighton's three from seven give approximately 42.9%. These are original calculations from the reported totals, not a complete ranking of either team's attacking quality.

The denominator matters. Saying “six versus three” compares the number of on-target efforts; saying “six of 21 versus three of seven” also describes the relationship to total attempts. Neither calculation includes every feature of the opportunities.

Do not infer that every off-target effort was equally wasteful or that every on-target effort was equally dangerous. Distance, angle, pressure and the position of defenders affect what a chance looks like. A count is useful precisely when its limits are understood.

## What expected goals can and cannot tell you

The same provider assigns City 1.92 expected goals and Brighton 1.0. Expected goals is a model-based description of chance quality, not an alternative final score or a debt that the next match must repay.

If you round those values to 2–1 and call that the result, you have changed the question. The official score remains 0–1. The model provides another way to discuss the chances created; it does not remove the goalkeeper, the actual finishes or the final whistle.

A helpful classroom comparison is to consider a set of opportunities whose model values add to more than one. That total does not mean one specific attempt must go in. It describes an expectation across the chances under the model, with real matches able to finish differently.

This article does not reconstruct the provider's model or claim that another data service must publish identical values. If comparing xG across websites, retain the provider name, fixture and version or date where available.

## Why a narrow score can involve several different stories

A 1–0 result tells us the winning margin, but not every phase of play. An early lead can change the decisions faced by both teams: the trailing side needs a goal, while the leading side must decide how to protect its advantage and use any opportunities to attack.

That is a general match-reading principle, not proof of every tactical instruction given in this fixture. To assess a specific sequence, watch the relevant passage and describe what is visible before guessing at the plan behind it.

For example, record where a team regains possession, which passing options appear and how the next action changes the space. This is more useful than applying a vague label such as “wanted it more” to every successful defensive effort.

Likewise, the losing side's larger attempt total does not make the winner's goal invalid or the match unfair. It invites a more precise question: how did the quality, execution and timing of opportunities differ?

## A practical table-reading exercise

Take the three published rows and verify four relationships. First, wins plus draws plus defeats should equal matches played. Second, goals for minus goals against should equal goal difference. Third, three points per win plus one per draw should match the points shown, assuming no applicable deduction. Fourth, every figure should belong to the same dated snapshot.

For Brighton, 6 + 1 + 2 equals nine matches. Twenty-four minus seven equals +17. Six wins multiplied by three, plus one draw, equals 19 points. These checks do not predict the season; they confirm that the row's basic arithmetic is internally consistent.
