This is a useful general distinction in sports reporting: expected, selected, started and performed are different states. They may occur in sequence, but one is not proof of the next. Do not convert a training report into a completed-match statistic.

It also explains why an all-rounder may be discussed as a batter. A player can possess bowling skills while being used only for batting in a particular period. A career label does not override a dated availability statement, and neither should be stretched into a permanent prediction.

## Why matches, innings and dismissals are different

In Test cricket, a team can have two innings in a match, but a particular player does not necessarily bat twice. A match might end earlier, or the team's innings might finish before the player is required. That is why dividing runs by matches is not the ordinary calculation for batting average.

A fictional example makes the difference clear. Suppose a player scores 300 runs across ten batting innings and remains not out twice. Runs per innings would be 30. Batting average would use eight dismissals as the denominator, giving 37.5. Both calculations can be correct if their labels are correct.

Now change the question to runs per match. You would need the number of matches as a separate input. It cannot be recovered simply by dividing the innings count by two. That shortcut assumes a pattern the record may not contain.

This is a transferable lesson for students: choose the denominator that answers your question. A school attendance rate, a cricket average and a website conversion rate all become misleading if the denominator changes without a corresponding change in the label.

## Do not add batting and bowling averages together

A batting average and a bowling average both use the word "average," but they describe different relationships. The first relates runs scored to dismissals. The second relates runs conceded to wickets taken. Adding them does not produce an accepted all-rounder quality score.

Instead, keep the dimensions visible. If your question concerns batting output, examine batting opportunities and results. If it concerns wicket-taking, examine bowling opportunities and wickets. If it concerns a team's balance, also ask which roles need covering in the particular match.

Imagine a fictional player contributes 45 runs but does not bowl because of the role assigned that day. Another contributes 15 runs and two wickets. You cannot fairly reduce the comparison to "45 is bigger than 15" and ignore the second skill. Equally, an unused bowling option is not two imaginary wickets waiting to be added.

A better comparison starts with a stated task. Who provided more batting runs in that match? The first player. Who took more wickets? The second. Which contribution was more useful overall? That requires match context and a reasoned argument rather than a convenient total of unlike units.

## Batting position is an opportunity, not a guarantee

A position in the order influences when a player may enter, but it does not specify the exact score or condition he will face. A number five can arrive during a collapse or after a substantial partnership. The position alone does not tell the entire story of the innings.

For a fictional comparison, place the same batter at number five in two scenarios: 35 for three and 280 for three. The title of the role is unchanged, but the scoreboard context differs considerably. A useful discussion would explain the difference rather than assuming the player has one identical job every time.

This is why reports about moving up or down the order should preserve their uncertainty. A suggested change is not a confirmed batting card. A confirmed position in one Test is not a promise for the next tour. Record the match and date whenever you make the comparison.

If you watch highlights, look for the score at entry and the dismissals already recorded. Those observable facts support a more specific explanation than simply saying a batter "rescued the team" or "had an easy situation" without examining the actual scorecard.

## Fielding deserves its own observation

Catches are valuable recorded events, but a catch total does not describe every fielding action. A stop, a relay, a throw or pressure that discourages a run can matter without becoming a catch in the statistical row. Avoid treating the available column as the entire skill.

For your own viewing, choose one fielder and follow the starting position before several deliveries. Note where the ball travels and whether an action is required. If the broadcast does not show the complete movement, record that limitation rather than assuming you saw the whole event.

A tall player's reach may be visually noticeable, but a photograph alone cannot establish a complete technical assessment. Do not invent a measurement of reaction speed, catching probability or injury risk from a still image. Specific observations are more useful than a confident conclusion built from appearance.

## Build a small all-rounder comparison project

Create a fictional dataset first, so you can test your method without accidentally publishing invented statistics about real players. Give each record a format, date range, batting innings, not-outs, runs, wickets and runs conceded. Leave a field empty when you do not know it rather than entering zero by default.

Then write a function that refuses an impossible denominator. If a record has more not-outs than innings, it needs correction. If a bowler has no wickets in the selected sample, do not divide by zero or display a fabricated numerical bowling average.

```python
def batting_average(runs, innings, not_outs):
    if min(runs, innings, not_outs) < 0 or not_outs > innings:
        raise ValueError('Check the batting record')
    dismissals = innings - not_outs
    return None if dismissals == 0 else runs / dismissals

print(batting_average(300, 10, 2))  # Fictional example: 37.5
```

The `None` result means the chosen formula has no numerical value for that record. It does not mean the player scored nothing. Explaining that distinction is part of presenting the data honestly, rather than hiding the problem behind a zero.

### Is Cameron Green a batter or an all-rounder?

His career includes batting and pace bowling, and the ICC profile labels him an all-rounder. A particular team's use of him can be narrower. Read dated availability and selection reports alongside the career profile instead of assuming the label guarantees a bowling role every day.

### Is this a current fitness announcement?

No. The availability discussion is explicitly tied to the 25 October 2026 report. This page does not diagnose an injury, announce clearance or forecast the next match. Use a newer official update for a newer availability question.

### Does an unbeaten score mean batting average is calculated per match?

No. Batting average uses dismissals, while runs per match uses appearances. An unbeaten innings affects the dismissal count. Keep the formula and label together so two different measurements are not presented as competing answers to the same question.
