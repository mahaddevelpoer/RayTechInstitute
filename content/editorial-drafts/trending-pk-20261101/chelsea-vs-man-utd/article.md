## Offside position is not automatically an offence

IFAB's Law 11 distinguishes being in an offside position from committing an offside offence. Position is assessed under the law's conditions, while an offence depends on the player's relevant involvement in active play. A still image showing a player nearer the goal than another player is not a complete explanation by itself.

The law also distinguishes the ball and the second-last opponent when defining position. The familiar shorthand "last defender" can be misleading because it substitutes a rough phrase for the actual reference points. Hands and arms are not treated like the relevant parts of the head, body and feet for this assessment.

For a beginner, the useful first question is which moment and action the decision concerns. Do not simply freeze the video when the scorer celebrates and assume that frame establishes the earlier position. Nor should you infer a precise official line from an oblique television image without the necessary information.

This guide does not independently redraw the decisions from the Chelsea match. It records what the official match report says and explains the broader distinction. ## A second yellow card and a penalty answer different questions

IFAB's Law 12 includes receiving a second caution in the same match among sending-off offences. A disciplinary sanction concerns the participant's conduct. A restart, including a penalty when appropriate under the law, concerns how play resumes. The two decisions are related but should be recorded separately.

In the Chelsea account, the incident involving Martinez and Wesley Fofana was reviewed, a penalty was awarded and Martinez received his second yellow card followed by red. That is the reported sequence. It does not justify a rule saying every penalty must come with a red card.

Use a fictional notebook with three fields: incident, disciplinary action and restart. If the incident leads to a caution and a free kick, write both. If a different incident leads to another decision, preserve that difference rather than assuming every foul has the same sanction.

Also distinguish a second-yellow dismissal from a straight red in your records. Both can result in a player being sent off, but they are not the same disciplinary path. A summary that simply says "red card" may be adequate for a headline, while a detailed event log should retain the distinction.

## What does playing against ten mean for analysis?

It describes a numerical situation after a dismissal, not a guarantee that every subsequent attack must succeed. A useful analysis would examine the period before and after the change, while recognising that the score and other events also change the situation.

Do not compare full-match totals as though every minute was played under identical conditions. If you are studying the effect of a dismissal, identify its timing and the score at that stage. A whole-game possession figure cannot isolate that effect on its own.

For a fictional exercise, imagine a side receives a red card in the tenth minute in one match and the eighty-fifth minute in another. Both results contain a sending-off, but the length of time spent short-handed is very different. Treating the two situations as identical would discard important context.

This does not mean you need elaborate statistics to discuss football. It means the claim should match the evidence. "The dismissal changed the numerical situation" is supported by the event. "Every later chance happened only because of it" would be a much stronger causal claim needing more analysis.

## Why the score does not tell the whole first-half story

A goalless interval can contain chances, saves and finishes that are disallowed. The score answers how many goals were confirmed by that stage. It does not measure how eventful the half felt or how many opportunities either side created.

This is why descriptions such as "nothing happened" need care. You might mean no goals were awarded, but the words imply a much broader absence of action. Use the narrow statement when that is what the record establishes.

For an original match-watching worksheet, create separate rows for confirmed goal, disallowed finish, save, shot wide and disciplinary event. Record a timestamp and a short observation. The categories make it easier to discuss an active half without confusing all noteworthy actions with goals.

Do not fill the worksheet from memory alone if accuracy matters. Recheck the recorded event or official account. A striking moment can remain vivid while its minute, order or final decision becomes blurred, especially when short clips are watched out of sequence.

## Build a score log that respects event status

A student programming project should store a goal's status explicitly rather than assume every event named "finish" counts. Use fictional inputs while learning the structure, so a test record is not accidentally published as a real match event.

```javascript
const events = [
  { team: 'Blue', kind: 'finish', awarded: false },
  { team: 'Red', kind: 'finish', awarded: false },
  { team: 'Blue', kind: 'finish', awarded: true },
  { team: 'Red', kind: 'card', awarded: false },
  { team: 'Blue', kind: 'finish', awarded: true },
];

const score = { Blue: 0, Red: 0 };
for (const event of events) {
  if (event.kind === 'finish' && event.awarded) {
    score[event.team] += 1;
  }
}
console.log(score); // Fictional event log: Blue 2, Red 0
```

The example is deliberately limited. A production match service would need event identifiers, corrections and reliable official inputs. The exercise teaches the first principle: a record's status is part of its meaning, and a card is not a goal just because it is an important event.
