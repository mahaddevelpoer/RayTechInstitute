Roma beat Udinese 5–2 at the Bluenergy Stadium on 31 October 2026, having led 4–0 at half-time. The home side scored twice after the interval, but Roma added a fifth in added time. That sequence makes this match a useful lesson in why a final score, a half-time score and a second-half score answer different questions.

## What was the Udinese versus Roma result?

The official Udinese report records Udinese 2–5 Roma and a half-time score of 0–4. In the usual home-first presentation, Udinese's two goals appear before Roma's five. Rewriting the result from the winner's perspective as “Roma won 5–2” describes the same match, provided the team names move with their scores.

This is an easy place to make a transcription error. If you copy only “2–5” into a table whose first column is Roma, you have reversed the result. Treat a score as two named values, not a decorative string. A reliable record says home team Udinese, home goals two, away team Roma, away goals five.

For a learner, the word “away” is also worth unpacking. It identifies Roma as the visiting team in this fixture. It does not imply the players were unfamiliar with the stadium or prove that crowd pressure caused an event. The fixture establishes who hosted; the explanation of performance needs further evidence.

Try reading the result aloud both ways. “Udinese lost two-five at home” and “Roma won five-two away” should preserve the same winner and margin. If your second version changes either, stop before calculating a table update. Many apparently complicated statistics errors begin with a simple failure to keep a label attached to its number.

## How did the goal sequence develop?

Udinese's report lists Roma goals from Cristante, Mora, Malen and Ndicka before half-time. Zaniolo and Palma scored for Udinese after the break, followed by Ghilardi for Roma at 90+2. The home report gives Palma's goal as minute 78; descriptions using another minute should be checked against the named provider rather than silently combined.

The important structure is a four-goal away lead, two home replies and a final away goal. A timeline communicates that structure more clearly than a list of seven names. Begin at 0–0, add each confirmed scoring event to the correct team, and check that the accumulated total ends at 2–5.

For a classroom version, use seven counters in two colours. Place the first four in Roma's column, the next two in Udinese's, and the last in Roma's. The final arrangement should match the result even when you hide the player names. This separates the arithmetic of the match from the task of remembering its scorers.

Do not add an assist, a type of finish or a tactical instruction just because the goal sequence leaves room for a story. Those details require their own evidence. You can explain the shape of the score accurately without claiming to have watched every angle or to know what the coaches said privately.

## Did Udinese win the second half?

Subtracting the recorded half-time score from the final score gives Udinese two second-half goals and Roma one. Udinese therefore outscored Roma 2–1 after the interval, while losing the complete match 2–5. “Won the second half” is informal shorthand for that segment, not a separate league victory or additional points award.

This distinction is useful because both observations can be true at once. A team can improve during one period without overturning the damage from another. Saying the home side scored more after half-time does not erase the first half, and saying Roma won comfortably on the scoreboard does not mean every period had the same score.

Draw two boxes, one for each half. Enter 0–4 in the first and 2–1 in the second. Add the team columns vertically: zero plus two gives Udinese two; four plus one gives Roma five. If you instead add across the rows, you learn how many goals occurred in each period, which is another valid but different quantity.

This simple matrix teaches a broader data skill. The same numbers can support several summaries, depending on which dimension you aggregate. Always state whether you are comparing teams, periods or the whole event. A correct addition with an unclear heading can still mislead the person trying to read your result.

## What does 90+2 mean on a goal timeline?

The notation identifies an event in second-half added time, two minutes beyond the regulation 90-minute reference. It is not a second match or an additional half. A timeline should preserve the notation rather than treating the plus sign as an unrelated symbol or replacing the event with an invented exact second.

There is also a difference between an event label and a full measurement of elapsed playing time. A report using 90+2 has not necessarily supplied the exact timestamp down to the second. Converting it into a precise duration can claim more accuracy than the source provides. Keep the original level of detail in a beginner's timeline.

Imagine sorting text labels alphabetically: “10,” “31,” “90+2” and “4” will not always behave like a proper chronological clock. In a small program, represent the period, minute and added-minute information explicitly. That makes it possible to display the familiar notation while preserving meaningful ordering behind the page.

For a first exercise, avoid handling every possible football period at once. Store this match's regulation goals and the confirmed added-time event, then explain your assumptions. You can expand the model later to extra time and shoot-outs without pretending those stages happened here. A useful model starts with the event actually being studied.

## What can the formations tell you?

The official report lists both sides with a 3-4-2-1 starting formation. That is a starting description, not proof that the teams occupied identical spaces or followed identical plans throughout the match. Shared formation numbers can coexist with very different movements, pressing choices and in-possession positions.

Read the numbers as a broad arrangement of outfield players: three, four, two and one add up to ten, with the goalkeeper outside that shorthand. This arithmetic is a first step, not a complete tactical analysis. A player can move between lines while remaining the same person in the starting eleven.

A helpful original activity is to draw ten markers for each team using the same starting arrangement. Then move one wide marker forward on one diagram and leave it deeper on the other. You have changed the picture without changing the starting formation label. The exercise illustrates a limitation of the notation, not a claim about a particular Roma attack.

Avoid turning the final score into automatic proof that one formation is superior. Both starting labels were the same here. Performance depends on execution and many other factors that a line of numbers cannot capture. Use formation as a map that introduces questions, rather than an answer that makes watching the match unnecessary.

## How large was the winning margin?

Roma's five goals minus Udinese's two gives a three-goal margin. The match contained seven goals in total. Margin and total goals are different measures: one describes the gap between the teams, while the other describes how often either side scored. Neither should be labelled as the other in a chart.

Try comparing an invented 3–0 result with this 5–2. Both have a three-goal margin, but they do not contain the same number of goals or the same defensive outcome. A margin-only chart hides that distinction. Add separate columns for goals scored and conceded if the question concerns attack and defence.

Goal difference for a season is also not simply the winning margin from the latest match. It accumulates goals for and against across the relevant league fixtures. This match contributes plus three to Roma's league goal difference and minus three to Udinese's, but it does not reveal either side's complete season figure by itself.

For a fictional table, give a team ten goals for and eight against before a 5–2 win. Afterwards, the totals become fifteen for and ten against, producing plus five overall. The match added plus three to a previous plus two. These invented starting totals are a calculation exercise, not either club's actual standing.

## Why did some fixture listings still look upcoming?

A scheduled fixture page and a completed match report serve different purposes and may update at different times. During research, some listings retained an upcoming-style display, while the clubs supplied a dated completed result. For the outcome, a completed official report is stronger evidence than an old schedule label.

This is not a reason to discard schedules entirely. A schedule helps identify competition, home team and planned date. A report confirms what happened. Match the same teams and season before connecting the two. Similar pairings recur, and a perfectly valid report from an earlier year will answer the wrong question if you overlook its date.

The same care applies to local time. A listing expressed in an Italian time zone and a page displaying a converted time may show different clock numbers without disagreeing about the event. Do not call that a scheduling error until you compare the time-zone labels. An unnamed clock time is incomplete information for an international reader.

For your own notebook, keep two fields: scheduled information and confirmed outcome. Update the latter from a dated report and retain where it came from. That makes a correction traceable instead of overwriting the evidence and leaving the next reader unable to tell why the record changed.

## Build a match-summary card that stays accurate

Start with the date, competition, home team, away team, half-time score and full-time score. Add the goal sequence only when you have a trustworthy list. Keep commentary in a separate paragraph from these core fields. Readers should be able to find the result without deciding which sentences are factual and which are interpretation.

Now test the card with three questions: who hosted, who won, and which side scored more after half-time? It should answer all three without contradiction. A card that says Roma won the match and Udinese outscored them after the break is not inconsistent. It is preserving two different levels of the same event.

## Check your understanding before sharing the result

Cover the first paragraph and reconstruct the match from your own card. If the card contains only 2–5, ask what information has gone missing. Without team labels and a date, the numbers could describe another fixture entirely. Put those fields back before using the result in a message, school presentation or social-media graphic.

Then ask a friend to interpret the half-time and full-time entries without help. If they think the two scores should be added together, explain that full-time already includes the first half. Adding 0–4 to 2–5 would count Roma's early goals twice. The correct second-half calculation is subtraction, not another addition of the complete match score.

Finally, separate certainty from opinion in one sentence each. “Roma won 5–2” is a confirmed result. “The match shows that Roma will win their next game” is a prediction that the score cannot prove. Learning to draw that boundary lets you enjoy football discussion while keeping a shared result card useful to readers who simply want accurate information.
