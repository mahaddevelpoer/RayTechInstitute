## Why did the side with more wickets lost still win?

The completed scores show that Afghanistan finished with more runs. Wickets are an innings resource and a record of dismissals, not points deducted directly from the run total. Losing four wickets does not erase the 121 runs already scored, just as losing only three does not add extra runs to Scotland's 100.

This is a common beginner confusion because scoreboard fields appear next to each other. A display such as 121/4 compresses two quantities into one familiar pattern. It does not mean 121 divided by four, and the second number is not a penalty that must be subtracted to find the result.

## How do runs, wickets and balls work together?

An innings has a current score and resources still available under the format's rules. Runs measure what has been added; wickets and legal balls help describe the remaining opportunity. None of these fields alone captures the whole state. A high score with little play left differs from the same score early in an innings.

## What does defending 121 mean?

Afghanistan batted first in the recorded match and Scotland's completed total remained lower. Describing Afghanistan as defending the total summarises that innings order and outcome. It does not tell you the field placements, every bowling plan or what the captain said privately before the chase.

A defending total creates a clear reference for the chasing side, subject to any applicable adjustments. In a straightforward unadjusted example, 121 implies 122 to win. The 21-run result margin comes from 121 minus 100; it should not be confused with the runs still needed to exceed the first total.

## Why does an unbeaten half-century need context?

Unbeaten means the batter was not dismissed in the recorded innings. A half-century identifies a run milestone. Neither word says how every run was scored or proves that the player faced the whole innings. Runs, balls faced and dismissal status should remain separate fields when you explain a performance.

In a fictional exercise, one batter makes fifty from twenty balls and another fifty from forty. Their totals match, but their scoring rates differ. Add not-out markers and the distinction still remains. The marker does not transform the rate into another quantity or tell you the team's full result.

The same principle applies to a short innings highlighted in a match report. Read the contribution within its stage and allocation. A burst in a six-over game should not be treated as an expectation that the same pace will continue over a much longer format. Samples and opportunities differ.

If you make a chart, label it as this innings rather than career performance. A bar representing one match is not a season trend. To describe consistency, you need multiple comparable observations, with their dates and formats. The desire for an impressive visual should not turn one notable contribution into a broader unsupported pattern.
