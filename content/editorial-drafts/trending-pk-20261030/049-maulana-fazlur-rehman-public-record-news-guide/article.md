## Check which parliamentary term you are reading

The National Assembly keeps profiles from different terms. An archived constituency or oath date can be accurate for that period while being unsuitable as a current description. Read the page's term and any archive warning before copying a title or constituency into a new article.

This guide links a specific profile rather than reproducing personal contact information. A public directory can support identity research without requiring an educational article to republish addresses and phone numbers.

## A speech is not an enacted policy

A politician may propose, support, criticise or negotiate a measure. Those actions should not be rewritten as a law already in force. For legislation, check the bill, parliamentary stage and official legal publication. For a party position, use a dated statement and identify who made it.

Short video clips can omit the question or surrounding sentence. Find the full recording or transcript before attributing a broad position from a few seconds of speech.

## Distinguish fact and analysis

A verified meeting date is a fact. A prediction about its effect on a coalition is analysis. Label the difference and avoid presenting a journalist's interpretation as an official announcement. Political disagreement does not justify unsupported allegations about a person.

## A useful civics exercise

Choose one public statement and make three notes: what was said, what official action followed and what remains uncertain. Attach a source to each. Compare the institutional process with the headline rather than deciding accuracy from agreement with a party.
