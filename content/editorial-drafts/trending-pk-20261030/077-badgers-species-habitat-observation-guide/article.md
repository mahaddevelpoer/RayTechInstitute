Badger is a common name applied to more than one animal. A European badger, an American badger and a honey badger should not be treated as interchangeable species. Start by identifying the animal and region before repeating a claim about appearance, diet, habitat or behaviour.

## Identify the species

The Wildlife Trusts' European badger page describes the familiar black-and-white striped animal found in Britain. That source is useful for that species and setting. It should not be silently applied to every animal called a badger elsewhere in the world.

A photograph needs a reliable caption. Similar common names and dramatic wildlife clips can make identification difficult, especially where the location is missing.

## Habitat and behaviour belong together

Food availability, shelter and disturbance influence where animals live and when they are active. A brief encounter does not establish a complete behavioural pattern. Observations collected over time provide better evidence than one unusual clip.

Distinguish a direct observation from an explanation. Seeing an animal near a garden does not prove that it caused every nearby mark or disturbance. Tracks and other signs require careful identification.

## Observe without interfering

Keep a respectful distance and do not approach a den, corner an animal or try to handle it. Wildlife rules differ by jurisdiction, so follow local authority guidance. This article does not provide instructions for trapping, feeding or keeping a wild animal.

Do not share a sensitive den location publicly. A photograph can be interesting without revealing enough detail to encourage disturbance. Use a responsible guide or an authorised viewing location where one is available.

## A nature-study task

Create a species card with a scientific name, range, habitat and two sourced characteristics. Add the date and region of each source. Then compare a second animal with a similar common name and explain one difference without relying on reputation alone.
