## Start with the innings summary

Read the total, wickets and overs first. In a chase, identify the target and any revision after an interruption. A team reaching a large score in its first innings has not won until the match is completed under the relevant playing conditions.

Overs use ball notation rather than ordinary decimals. For example, 24.3 means twenty-four overs and three legal deliveries. This matters when calculating scoring rates or deciding how much of an innings remains.

## Follow batting and partnerships

Runs, balls faced, boundaries and strike rate show different parts of an innings. A partnership can explain how the total developed, especially after early wickets. Include extras in the team calculation and recognise that not every run belongs to a batter.

A batter marked not out should not be treated as dismissed. Retirement, absence and other scorecard labels also need their own meaning. Check the report rather than guessing from a blank dismissal column.

## Read the bowling and fielding record

Overs, maidens, conceded runs and wickets help describe a spell. Run-outs do not count as wickets to the bowler. A catch identifies the fielder involved but does not show the difficulty of the chance. Commentary and authorised video can add context that the table alone cannot provide.

## Confirm the official result

Distinguish a final result from an in-progress score. Rain, a tie or an abandonment can change how the outcome is recorded.
