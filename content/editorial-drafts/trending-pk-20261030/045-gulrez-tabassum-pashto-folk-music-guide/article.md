## Listen to the words and the delivery

Folk music is not only a tune. Pronunciation, repetition, rhythm and the singer's phrasing help communicate a text. A listener learning Pashto can begin with a short authorised recording and note familiar words before seeking a reliable explanation of unfamiliar expressions.

Do not assume a literal translation captures every cultural reference. A phrase can carry a local meaning, an idiom or a poetic convention. Credit a translator where one is used and distinguish their interpretation from the original wording.

## Identify recordings accurately

Check the performer, title and source before sharing a clip. Reuploads may omit credits or attach a familiar singer's name to someone else's performance. A video thumbnail is not sufficient proof of authorship, recording date or ownership.

Prefer an authorised broadcaster, rights holder or documented archive. An old recording can be culturally valuable without being free to copy. Public availability online does not remove copyright in the music, words or recording.

## Study without flattening a tradition

Pashto-speaking communities have varied musical experiences. One artist or style should not be presented as the voice of every listener. Explain the particular recording you are discussing rather than making unsupported claims about an entire culture.

Students can compare tempo, repeated phrases and vocal emphasis across two short excerpts without copying the lyrics into a project. Record where the performances came from and what remains uncertain about their history.

## Preserve useful context

When a family or teacher shares a recording, note its title, performer and known source. Avoid adding a dramatic biography simply to make a tribute feel complete.
