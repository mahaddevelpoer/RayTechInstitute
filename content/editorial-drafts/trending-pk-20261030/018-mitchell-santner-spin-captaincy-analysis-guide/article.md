A captain can influence a match through field placement and bowling changes even when their own figures do not tell the whole story.

## Watch the plan around the delivery

For a spinner, line and length interact with the field. Boundary protection can invite a single while discouraging a high-risk shot. A close catcher may show an attacking intention. Look at the batter's strengths, the required run rate and the stage of the innings before deciding what the bowler was trying to achieve.

Economy rate is useful, but it is not a complete measure of pressure. Dot balls, changes of pace and the difficulty of scoring in a particular area can influence the next shot. A wicket at the other end may be partly shaped by pressure built earlier.

## Captaincy needs context

A bowling change should be judged against the options available at the time. Workload, remaining overs and a batter's handedness can all matter. An expensive over does not by itself prove that the decision was unreasonable, just as a fortunate wicket does not make every plan correct.

Avoid assuming that a past captaincy announcement applies to every future format or squad. The linked ICC release concerns a particular tour arrangement. Selection and leadership responsibilities can change.

## Separate statistics by format

Test, ODI and T20I records answer different questions. A long defensive spell in a Test cannot be judged against a short white-ball role without explaining the differences. Record the date of any career average or ranking you cite, and do not use an old cached figure as a live update.

## Try a field-setting exercise

Pause an authorised match recording before a delivery. Sketch the visible field, predict the intended scoring restriction and then watch the outcome. Write what you observed separately from what you inferred. This is more instructive than judging captaincy only after the result.
