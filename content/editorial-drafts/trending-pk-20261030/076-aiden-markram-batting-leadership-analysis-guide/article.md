A captain can influence field settings and bowling changes, while the same player's innings responds to a different set of immediate demands.

## Establish the role for that match

Check the format, team sheet and captaincy designation. A previous leadership role does not automatically apply to every later squad. Use a dated board announcement for current responsibilities rather than treating a historical achievement as a permanent title.

Batting position also matters. Opening, rebuilding in the middle order and finishing an innings offer different opportunities and risks. Record the entry score and available overs before comparing totals.

## Read shot selection in context

Observe the delivery, field and scoring requirement. A boundary can follow a well-created opportunity or an unusually risky option. The outcome alone does not settle the quality of the decision. Watch complete overs where possible, since highlights omit the setup between dramatic moments.

Compare like formats and a stated sample. A handful of innings can show possibilities without proving a stable long-term pattern.

## Leadership is not hindsight

A bowling change should be evaluated against the available options at the time. Remaining overs, matchups and workload can influence the decision. A plan that fails can still have a defensible logic; a fortunate wicket does not make every choice correct.

Separate an observed field change from a guessed motive. If the recording does not show the instruction, present the explanation as an interpretation rather than a quotation.

## A balanced review exercise

Write one paragraph on batting, one on a documented leadership decision and one on the limitations of your evidence. Support each with a scorecard or authorised video.
