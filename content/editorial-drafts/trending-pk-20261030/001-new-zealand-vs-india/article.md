## How to read the batting scorecard

The scorecard offers more context than the headline total. Look at the batting order, runs scored and balls faced. The strike rate expresses runs per hundred balls; it should be interpreted alongside the phase of the innings and the number of wickets available.

A short, fast contribution can help a chase without being the highest individual score. A slower passage may reflect a recovery after wickets. Neither conclusion should be drawn from a single number without looking at the surrounding deliveries and partnerships.

Boundaries and dot balls help explain changing pressure. Several deliveries without a run can raise the asking rate, while a sequence of fours or sixes can bring it down. The useful question is what changed in the match situation, not simply which batter has the largest total.

## Bowling figures, extras and overs

Compare wickets taken, runs conceded and overs bowled. The economy rate describes scoring against a bowler, but a spell during the powerplay is not necessarily comparable with one in the death overs. Fielding restrictions, attacking intent and the situation affect how the figures should be read.

Six legal balls make an over. A notation such as 12.4 means twelve completed overs and four legal deliveries, not twelve point four as an ordinary decimal. This distinction matters when calculating how many balls remain.

Extras also influence the total. Wides and no-balls can change both the score and the legal-delivery count, while byes and leg-byes are recorded separately in the detailed figures. A close result can make these differences important, so inspect the complete innings rather than a cropped score graphic.

## Understanding the target and required run rate

During a chase, identify the target, required run rate, balls remaining and wickets in hand. A team may maintain a respectable current run rate while still needing to accelerate. Another side may score slowly for a period but preserve enough batting resources to attack later.

A partnership can stabilise the chase, but its value also depends on the remaining target. Avoid treating one big over as proof that the contest is settled. Pressure can change quickly when a wicket follows a boundary or a new batter needs time to adjust.

If weather reduces the available overs, look for an officially revised target. Do not announce a winner by comparing totals from different playing conditions without checking the match centre's current decision and status.
